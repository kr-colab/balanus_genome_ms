\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Genome assembly}

% General stats on assembly. Confirming the quality and status of the genome.

We assembled a chromosome-level reference genome for the Pacific acorn barnacle
using PacBio HiFi long reads and Hi-C chromosome conformation capture. Preliminary
assessment of the PacBio HiFi data using k-mers revealed an expected genome size
of around 845 Mbp, with a heterozygosity of over 3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_kmers}).
Our initial contig-level assembly, generated using \textsc{hifiasm}
\citep{cheng_haplotype-resolved_2021,cheng_haplotype-resolved_2022} with
default parameters, produced sequences with a total length of 1.64 Gbp,
composed of 4,212 fragments, and a contig N50 of 734 kbp. We also assessed
gene-completeness using \textsc{BUSCO}s \citep{simao_busco_2015,manni_busco_2021,
tegenfeldt_orthodb_2025}, as implemented in \textsc{compleasm}
\citep{huang_compleasm_2023}, resulting in a completeness of 95.66\%
with over 50\% duplicated orthologs
for the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} single-copy ortholog set
(Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). The statistics for this initial assembly, with
twice the expected genome size and over 50\% duplicated \textsc{BUSCO}s,
suggested the presence of a large fraction of haplotig sequences, reflecting
the high heterozygosity observed for the species.
\vspace{0.2cm}

After optimizing the assembly with the addition of ONT ultra-long reads and more stringent
purging of haplotig and contaminant sequences (see \nameref{sec:methods_assembly}), we
generated contigs with a substantially smaller fraction of haplotigs, reducing the
assembly size to 1.05 Gbp and the \textsc{BUSCO} duplicates to 4.84\%.
This contig-level assembly was then scaffolded with Hi-C data using the \textsc{YaHS}
software \citep{zhou_yahs_2023} (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_hic}), generating an assembly
with a total length of 1.05 Gbp, composed of 650 scaffolds, a scaffold N50
of 50.96 Mbp, and a \textsc{BUSCO} completeness of 93.98\% (4.74\% duplicated;
\texttt{arthropoda\_odb10}) (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

This scaffold-level assembly was further processed by performing manual curation
of the Hi-C contact map and polishing using the software \textsc{Inspector}
\citep{chen_accurate_2021}. This curated assembly was composed of 592 fragments,
a total sequence length of 1.04 Gbp, a scaffold N50 of 51.41 Mbp, and a per-base
quality value (QV) of 30.49. When assessing completeness, the curated assembly was
94.08\% and 92.77\% gene-complete for the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and
\texttt{crustacea\_odb12} \textsc{BUSCO} datasets, respectively
(Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). Of the 1.04 Gbp of total sequence,
906.6 Mbp (86.74\%) was contained in 58 scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp. Moreover,
the 16 largest sequences (larger than 8.5 Mbp) contained 841.2 Mbp (80.61\%) of
the total sequence, and likely represent 16 chromosomes. This karyotype
number, N=16, is in agreement with that observed for other species in the genus
\textit{Balanus} \citep{austin_chromosome_1958} and other acorn barnacles in the
family Balanidae \citep{han_new_2024}.
Together, the high contiguity, gene-completeness, and chromosome-level scaffolding
of this assembly make it among the most complete genomic resources available for
any barnacle species, and a strong foundation for downstream population genomic
and comparative analyses.

\subsection*{Genome annotation}

% Stats and QC on the annotation.

Once the assembly and scaffolding were completed, we annotated and masked repeat
sequences in the \bgla{} reference assembly using \textsc{EarlGrey}
\citep{baril_earl_2024}. This analysis identified 698.6 Mbp (66.94\%)
of the assembly as repetitive elements (Table~\ref{tab:sup_earlgrey_summary});
however, the majority of these, 404.5 Mbp (38.76\% of the assembly), remained
unclassified among the major repeat element types. This large proportion of
unclassified repeat elements is likely the product of the small number of
representative barnacle sequences among curated repeat reference libraries,
reflecting the status of barnacles as a genomically understudied group. Among the
classified repeats, DNA and LINE elements were the two most abundant repeat
classes, comprising 8.45\% and 9.16\% of the total assembly, respectively.

To assess large-scale structural conservation across barnacle genomes, we performed
a synteny analysis using the software \textsc{Genespace}
\citep{lovell_genespace_2022}. We compared our \bgla{} genome against the
chromosome-level genome assemblies of the striped barnacle \textit{Amphibalanus
amphitrite} \citep[Balanidae;][]{han_new_2024} and the gooseneck barnacle
\textit{Pollicipes pollicipes} \citep[Pollicipedidae;][]{bernot_chromosome-level_2022}.
These genomes display conservation in their large-scale organization, including
one-to-one correspondence between 16 putatively orthologous chromosomes across the
three species (Figure~\ref{fig:synteny}). This finding shows that our scaffolding
of the \bgla{} assembly likely reflects the biological structure of
the genome and is not the product of technical artifacts, and further suggests
conservation of genome architecture across barnacles spanning $\approx$165 Mya
of evolution \citep{han_new_2024,bernot_chromosome-level_2022}.
This striking chromosomal stability provides a shared framework for comparative
genomic analyses across the order and suggests that large-scale genome
rearrangements have not been a major driver of diversification in barnacles.

\subsection*{High genetic diversity in the \balgla{} reference assembly}

We estimated the genetic diversity present in the \bgla{} genome by identifying
heterozygous variants in our reference individual. After filtering low-quality sites
and masking regions with annotated repeats, we obtained genotypes for 314 million
sites along the genome, equivalent to 34.71\% of the 904.94 Mbp present in the 58
scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp (Table~\ref{tab:sup_popgen_stats}). Among these sites, 
9.08 million (2.89\%) were heterozygous in the reference individual, including 8.73
million SNPs and 356 thousand indels.
In total, this variation represents a mean density of 14.66 heterozygous sites per kbp
(median: 6 per kbp; standard deviation: 19.86). Moreover, we observed nearly 400
thousand variant sites within protein-coding sequences, including 144 thousand
non-synonymous point mutations, as categorized by the \textsc{snpEff} software
\citep{cingolani_program_2012}. In this reference individual, transcripts had a mean
of 7.74 (median: 3, standard deviation: 14.97) non-synonymous variants, indicating
considerable coding variation segregating within a single individual.
Even from this single genome, the sheer volume of heterozygosity underscores
the exceptionally large population sizes of this species and foreshadows
the extreme diversity observed at the population level.

\subsection*{Functional elements are conserved in barnacle genomes}

% For phastCons, mostly saying how we found the hughly conserved stuff and what
% it is. The enrichment results are generally expected (tRNAs and CDSs are conserved).

We used the \textsc{phastCons} package \citep{siepel_evolutionarily_2005} on the
\textsc{cactus} whole-genome alignment \citep{armstrong_progressive_2020} between
six barnacle species to identify highly conserved sites in the Pacific acorn barnacle
genome. Across the 16 putative chromosomes in the \textit{B. glandula} assembly,
\textsc{phastCons} identified 196,141 highly conserved elements (HCEs), spanning
50.85 Mbp (6.05\%) of the 16 assembled chromosomes.
In \textit{B. glandula}, the HCEs had a mean length of 259.25 bp (median: 42 bp,
standard deviation: 990.40 bp).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following the identification of HCEs, we compared their sequence composition against
the genome-wide background (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}).
We first intersected the genomic coordinates of the HCEs with the \bgla{}
annotation, obtaining the proportion of annotated features among HCE sites
(Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}A). Among our target features — which include an
``intergenic'' class for the unannotated fraction of the genome and a ``multiple''
class for annotation overlaps (e.g., a site spanning both a transposable element
and an intron) — all but one are present among HCEs. We did not observe any conserved
rRNA sites; however, this is likely the product of the whole-genome alignment, given
rRNA's repetitive nature and alignment difficulty \citep{gillespie_characterizing_2004},
and not an accurate measure of conservation (or lack thereof).
Aside from the absence of rRNAs, the sequence composition of HCEs largely followed that of
the whole genome.
Most annotated features do not appear substantially enriched among HCE sites
(Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}B, Table~\ref{tab:sup_phastcons_enrichment}).
The two clear exceptions are coding sequences (CDSs) and tRNAs.
Protein-coding sequences display an approximately 2-fold enrichment among HCEs (mean
$\log_2$ enrichment: 0.75, median: 0.98, standard deviation: 0.93), reflecting the
conservation of protein-coding genes among barnacle genomes.
Similarly, tRNAs exhibit $\approx$4-fold enrichment in HCEs (mean $\log_2$ enrichment: 2.14,
median: 2.04, standard deviation: 0.96), likely reflecting the strong functional constraints
on these sequences.
\vspace{0.2cm}

The rank order of enrichment among conserved elements in \bgla{} mirrors patterns
observed across other groups of organisms. \cite{siepel_evolutionarily_2005} characterized
the distribution of \textsc{phastCons}-conserved elements across vertebrate, insect, worm,
and yeast genomes, finding that coding sequences are consistently enriched among conserved
elements, with enrichment values ranging from 15.5-fold in vertebrates to lower values in
the more compact insect and worm genomes. A subsequent analysis of 20 angiosperm plant
genomes using the same framework \citep{hupalo_conservation_2013} found that translational
RNAs were the most enriched annotation among conserved regions ($\approx$3.6-fold),
followed by coding sequences ($\approx$1.3-fold), while transposable elements were
drastically underrepresented. The enrichment pattern in barnacles follows the same ordering
observed in both plants and animals: tRNAs are the most enriched feature
($\approx$4-fold), followed by CDSs ($\approx$2-fold), while transposable elements
are depleted among HCEs.

\vspace{0.2cm}

As fellow arthropods, insects provide the most phylogenetically relevant comparison for
barnacles.
In \textit{Drosophila}, conserved elements cover 44.5\% of the genome, with 34\% of
conserved bases falling in protein-coding exons \citep{siepel_evolutionarily_2005}.
Less dramatic differences in enrichment across annotation classes are also observed when
compared with vertebrate genomes.
In \bgla{}, HCEs cover a substantially smaller fraction of the genome (6.05\%), and only
4.4\% of HCE bases overlap CDSs (Table~\ref{tab:sup_phastcons_enrichment}).
Both of these values are lower than those observed in insects; however, this difference is
likely driven by the repeat-rich nature of the barnacle genome (67\% repetitive elements,
compared with $\approx$15\% in \textit{D. melanogaster}), which both makes aligning these
genomes more difficult while also contributing to a larger fraction of HCE bases in
transposable element and overlapping annotation categories.
Despite these differences in raw composition, CDSs are enriched among conserved elements
in both insects and barnacles, consistent with the expectation that protein-coding
sequences are among the most functionally constrained regions in arthropod genomes.
More broadly, \cite{siepel_evolutionarily_2005} showed that the proportion of conserved
bases that overlap protein-coding exons scales with genome compactness, from 86\% in
yeast to 59\% in worms, 34\% in insects, and 28\% in vertebrates, with plants at 42\%
\citep{hupalo_conservation_2013}.
The low CDS proportion in barnacle HCEs extends this trend to an arthropod with a much
larger, more repeat-rich genome than the model insects examined in previous studies.
Additionally, introns are mildly enriched among barnacle HCEs
($\approx$1.3-fold), in contrast with their depletion in plant ($\approx$0.4-fold;
\citealp{hupalo_conservation_2013}), insect, and vertebrate conserved elements
\citep{siepel_evolutionarily_2005}.
However, it remains unknown if this enrichment reflects the larger intronic fraction
of the barnacle genome or genuine intronic conservation in this lineage.
Overall, the conservation of functional elements across barnacle genomes indicates
that purifying selection has been effective at maintaining these sequences despite
the highly repetitive nature of the genome, a pattern we revisit in the context
of the broader selective regime acting on these large populations.

\subsection*{Patterns of molecular evolution reflect positive selection in protein-coding genes}

To examine the rate of molecular evolution in protein-coding sequences, we calculated
the pairwise ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (\dnds) between
\bgla{} and its congener, the wrinkled barnacle \textit{Balanus crenatus},
using the codon counting approach described by \cite{nei1986_dnds}.
We identified and aligned 9,082 single-copy orthologs between the two species.
The mean pairwise \dnds{} was 0.206 (median: 0.158, standard deviation: 0.191;
Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dnds}). This ratio indicates that, on average, coding
sequences in these barnacle genomes are under purifying selection, reflecting the
expected evolutionary constraints on sequences of functional importance.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Of the nine thousand genes assessed, 70 (0.77\%) displayed a \dnds{} $>$ 1, and are likely
candidates for positive selection.
The predicted function of the majority ($>80\%$) of these genes is unknown, as
determined by the functional annotation generated by the \textsc{EnTAP} software
\citep{hart_span_2020}.
This is expected, since homology is likely harder to determine for fast-evolving sequences
putatively under strong selection.
Regardless, we found several genes with known function in our candidates under
positive selection, among them a follistatin-A-like homolog, known to be involved in
myogenesis and muscle development in crustaceans \citep{yan_identification_2021}.
We also observed a homolog of gamma-interferon-inducible lysosomal thiol reductase,
which plays a role in crustacean innate immune response \citep{liu_involvement_2019}.
Additionally, we found evidence of positive selection in homologs of tumor protein
p53-inducible protein 11, which is involved in cell cycle regulation and is up-regulated
in crabs after viral infection \citep{cheng_p53_2021}, as well as quiver gene homologs,
related to neuronal activity and sleep regulation in \textit{Drosophila}
\citep{wang_quiver_2010}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We expanded on our \dnds{} measures of positive selection in protein-coding sequences
in the \bgla{} genome by incorporating population-level polymorphism using the
McDonald-Kreitman (MK) test \citep{mcdonald_adaptive_1991}.
Specifically, we used polymorphism data from six \bgla{} individuals from the central
Oregon coast (see below), comparing those against the orthologous coding sequences in
the \textit{B. crenatus} outgroup.
After removing alignments shorter than 100 bp and those containing premature stop
codons or frameshift mutations, we calculated the MK test for 7,374 orthologous
sequences between \bgla{} and \textit{B. crenatus}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Four hundred and forty-four (444, 6.02\%) of these orthologs showed a significant
deviation from the expected proportion of non-synonymous and synonymous substitutions
and polymorphisms, following \textit{p}-value correction for multiple testing
(Figure~\ref{fig:mkTest}).
We additionally calculated the Neutrality Index (NI;~\citealp{rand_excess_1996}) and
Direction of Selection (DoS;~\citealp{stoletzki_estimation_2011}) at each ortholog.
We observed a mean NI value of 0.745 (median: 0.554; standard deviation: 1.079) and
a mean DoS of 0.102 (median: 0.106; standard deviation: 0.131; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dos}).
Both of these statistics show that on average, and in contrast with our measurements
of \dnds{} (see \nameref{sec:discussion_dnds_mkt}), protein-coding sequences in \bgla{}
exhibit an excess of non-synonymous substitutions, suggesting that they are evolving
under positive directional selection when compared to the outgroup sequence.
\vspace{0.2cm}

In addition to NI and DoS, we calculated $\alpha$, a measure of the proportion
of non-synonymous substitutions fixed by positive selection \citep{smith_adaptive_2002}.
We calculated two adjusted versions of $\alpha$ to account for the presence of known
biases.
First, we calculated the Tarone-Greenland $\alpha$ estimator ($\alpha_\text{TG}$), a
weighted estimator that corrects for the effects of averaging over a large number of
genes \citep{stoletzki_estimation_2011}.
Across our 7,374 genes, we calculated an $\alpha_\text{TG}$ of 0.540 ($95\%$ CI:
0.534--0.550), which indicates that $\approx$ 54\% of non-synonymous substitutions in
this \bgla{} population were fixed as a product of positive selection.
Secondly, we calculated $\alpha$ using the asymptotic MK test ($\alpha_\text{asym}$),
which corrects for the presence of weakly deleterious non-synonymous polymorphisms
present at low frequency in the population \citep{messer_frequent_2013} which might
obscure the signal of positive selection.
We found a mean $\alpha_\text{asym}$ of 0.610 ($95\%$ CI: 0.610--0.622), a
larger proportion than that observed in \textit{Drosophila} ($\alpha_\text{asym}$
of 0.57; \citealp{haller_asymptoticmk_2017}).
Regardless of the metric used, both $\alpha_\text{TG}$ and $\alpha_\text{asym}$ agree
that the majority of non-synonymous substitutions in this barnacle population are the
outcome of directional positive selection.
Notably, this pervasive signal of positive selection stands in apparent tension with
the low genome-wide \dnds{}, a discordance we address in the \nameref{sec:discussion}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Exploring the predicted function of the candidate genes identified in the MK analysis,
our single candidate displaying an excess of polymorphism relative to divergence is a
homolog of tubulin alpha-1C chain-like, a major constituent of microtubules
which has been observed as differentially expressed in response to osmotic stress in
barnacles \citep{chandramouli_transcriptome_2015}.
While its exact function in \bgla{} is unknown, the large excess of polymorphism
observed (NI = 18.54) suggests strong balancing selection acting on this gene in
this system.

\subsection*{Non-neutral evolution in the classic allozyme loci}

In the Atlantic acorn barnacle \textit{Semibalanus balanoides}, a set of allozyme loci
have been extensively linked to spatial heterogeneity and local adaptation
\citep{flowerdew_electrophoretic_1983,nunez_footprints_2020,rand_ecological_2002,
schmidt_environmental_2000,flight_allozyme_2012,veliz_regional_2004}.
Prior allozyme work in \bgla{} itself is more limited but also revealing:
\cite{hedgecock_temporal_1994} surveyed five polymorphic loci (including
\textit{Mpi} and \textit{Gpi}) across ten localities from central California to
Alaska, and while broad-scale differentiation was low ($\text{F}_\text{LT} = 0.023$),
four of the five loci showed slight but statistically significant allele-frequency
heterogeneity at microgeographic scales --- including heterogeneity at \textit{Mpi}
across a single 1-m intertidal transect at Bodega Harbor.
These allozyme-era observations left open the question of whether the microgeographic
signal reflects protein-level selection, drift among sweepstakes-limited recruitment
cohorts \citep{hedgecock_does_1994}, or some combination of the two.
With chromosome-level assemblies now available for both \bgla{} and \textit{B. crenatus},
together with population-level polymorphism data, we can revisit these classic allozyme
loci at molecular resolution.
To do so, we identified orthologs to seven of these classic allozyme loci
in our assembly (Table~\ref{tab:sup_allozymes}), including
alcohol dehydrogenase (\textit{Adh}), glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (\textit{Gpi}), two
different copies of the NADP-dependent malic enzyme (\textit{Me.1} and \textit{Me.2}),
mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (\textit{Mpi}), phosphoglucomutase (\textit{Pgm}), and
superoxide dismutase (\textit{Sod}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

When compared against the \textit{B. crenatus} orthologous sequences, all seven loci show
\dnds{} less than one (Table~\ref{tab:sup_allozymes_dnds}), in agreement with our genome-wide
estimates, suggesting that they are evolving under purifying selection (but see
\nameref{sec:discussion_dnds_mkt}).
Assessing these loci using the MK test reveals different selection regimes acting on these
loci.
Among the seven allozyme loci, only \textit{Gpi} and \textit{Me.2} show a significant MK
test result (corrected $p < 0.05$), showing deviation from the expected number of synonymous
and non-synonymous divergence and polymorphism under neutrality
(Table~\ref{tab:sup_allozymes_mkt}).
For both of these genes, both the NI and DoS statistics show that this deviation is due to
an excess of non-synonymous substitutions, indicating that these sequences evolved under
a regime of positive selection.
The $\alpha$ statistic additionally shows that the majority of non-synonymous
substitutions in these two genes, 76.2\% for \textit{Gpi} and 71.9\% for \textit{Me.2},
reached fixation as the product of directional positive selection.
Both estimates sit meaningfully above the genome-wide asymptotic estimate
($\alpha_\text{asym} = 0.610$), placing these two classic allozyme loci among the more
rapidly evolving
protein-coding sequences in the \bgla{} genome.
Among the remaining loci, \textit{Mpi} shows the largest excess of polymorphism
relative to divergence (NI = 1.575), though the MK test is not significant after
correction ($p = 0.443$).
Examination of synonymous diversity ($\pi_{4}$) relative to synonymous divergence
($\text{K}_\text{S}$) in sliding windows along each allozyme coding sequence reveals
localized elevations at all seven loci, with outlier windows tending to fall within
single exons (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_gw_piks}; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_allozyme_piks}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

To test the seven allozyme loci for departures from neutrality at the level of within-species
polymorphism relative to between-species divergence, we applied the Hudson-Kreitman-Aguad\'{e}
test \citep{hudson_test_1987}. For each locus we assembled a full-length alignment spanning
the gene body plus 5\,kb of flanking sequence on either side, stitching the codon-aware
\bgla{} / \textit{B. crenatus} coding alignment together with pairwise alignment of introns
and intergenic flanks, and augmented with twelve per-sample \bgla{} ingroup haplotypes
derived from our population panel. Testing each allozyme locus against the pooled remainder
under the segregating-sites variant of the test (Table~\ref{tab:sup_hka}), only \textit{Me.1}
showed a nominally significant deviation ($p = 0.032$, uncorrected for multiple
comparisons across seven loci), exhibiting a deficit of polymorphism relative to its
divergence --- the direction expected under a recent selective sweep or strong purifying
constraint at linked sites.
\vspace{0.2cm}

The two MK outliers, \textit{Gpi} and \textit{Me.2}, did not reach significance individually
under the classic HKA test, but both showed direction-consistent excess polymorphism
relative to divergence. To visualize where along each locus the signal concentrates we
applied the sliding-window HKA diagnostic \citep{kreitman_inferring_1991}, which
displays local departures from the neutral expectation but does not yield a formal
significance threshold, using a window of 500 silent sites centered on each nucleotide
position (Figure~\ref{fig:sliding_hka}).
Both \textit{Me.2} and \textit{Pgm} show observed silent diversity exceeding the
divergence-scaled neutral expectation across multiple sub-regions of the gene body, with
the largest excesses localized to coding and intronic windows rather than spread
uniformly. These localized peaks are consistent with selection acting
heterogeneously along the gene body at these loci (see Figure~\ref{fig:sup_sliding_hka}
for the full set of seven loci).

\subsection*{Barnacle genes show a biased use of synonymous codons}

We used the annotated transcript sequences of our Pacific acorn barnacle genome
to calculate the effective number of codons (ENC), a measure of the extent of
departure from equal usage across synonymous codons \citep{wright_effective_1990,
sun_improved_2013}. We calculated ENC across the coding sequences of
16,904 \bgla{} genes, finding a mean of 43.1 effective codons (median: 42.7,
standard deviation: 5.56, Figure~\ref{fig:codon_enc}). This ENC distribution
indicates a codon usage bias stronger than that observed in \textit{Drosophila}
(Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dmel_enc}), suggesting that a large portion of synonymous mutations
may not be neutral in this large barnacle population.

\subsection*{Barnacle populations exhibit elevated genetic polymorphism}

We sequenced the whole genomes of eight \bgla{} individuals from the central Oregon coast.
After removal of two samples with poor quality sequences, and the stringent filtering of
sites with low coverage, low quality, and/or missing genotypes, we retained a total of
111.6 million genotyped sites (Table~\ref{tab:sup_popgen_stats}), 21.4 million (19.16\%)
of which were variant in the retained individuals.
Among these variant sites, we recovered 20.5 million point mutations, 4.86\% of which 
were multiallelic in just these six individuals.
In total, the amount of polymorphism observed in our sampling translates to a mean
density of 41.05 variant sites per kbp (median: 29, standard deviation: 40.82).
Moreover, 1.12 million of these variant sites (5.23\%) were located in coding sequences.
These coding variants included 360 thousand missense (non-synonymous) mutations,
corresponding to a mean of 18.93 non-synonymous variants per transcript
(median: 10, standard deviation: 28.50).
Overall, these represent a substantial proportion of coding variation segregating in
this central Oregon population.
This \bgla{} population displayed a mean genome-wide nucleotide
diversity ($\pi$) of 0.0506 (median: 0.0531, standard deviation: 0.0188;
Figure~\ref{fig:genome_pi}; Table~\ref{tab:sup_feature_pi}), a degree of diversity that
is an order of magnitude higher than that observed in diverse \textit{Drosophila}
populations in Sub-Saharan Africa (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dmel_pi}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Along each chromosome, we did not observe any strong correlation between the density
of specific genomic features (e.g., CDSs, lncRNAs, tRNAs, introns, and repeats) and
$\pi$, particularly not at the scale of 10 kbp (Figure~\ref{fig:genome_pi};
Figure~\ref{fig:sup_pi_pairplot}). However, stronger relationships are likely present
at smaller genomic intervals (see below). The strongest correlation was, as expected,
observed between the density of segregating sites within 10 kbp windows and $\pi$
(Spearman's $\rho$: 0.706; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_pi_pairplot}). The proportion of
segregating sites in itself was not correlated with the density of particular genomic
features, except for repeats; however, this is likely an artifact of the masking of
genotypes located within repetitive intervals in the reference
(see \nameref{sec:methods_popgen}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Looking at diversity across the genome, we observed an overall reduction in $\pi$
at putatively functional sites. For example, the mean $\pi$ in sequences spanning
annotated CDSs is 0.0302 (median: 0.0270, standard deviation: 0.0221;
Figure~\ref{fig:feature_pi}; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_pi_hist}; Table~\ref{tab:sup_feature_pi}),
which represents a $\approx$ 1.6x reduction in diversity when compared to the
genome-wide average. A reduction in diversity of similar magnitude is also observed
in the sequences of lncRNAs (1.4x) and 5' UTRs (1.3x). The largest reduction in
diversity when compared to the genome-wide background was observed in tRNAs, with
$\pi$ $\approx$ 4.3x lower than the genomic background and consistent with the strong
functional constraint evidenced by their enrichment in highly conserved elements. In
contrast to most other elements of the genome, introns display a relative increase in
diversity, with a mean $\pi$ of 0.0646 (median: 0.0650, standard deviation: 0.0299),
approximately 1.3x higher than the genome-wide average.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Zooming in further on coding sequences, we explored the patterns of $\pi$
at 0-fold degenerate ($\pi_{0}$), in which any mutation produces a non-synonymous
substitution, and 4-fold degenerate sites ($\pi_{4}$), in which any mutation results
in a synonymous change.
This comparison allows us to compare diversity at constrained versus neutral (or
putatively neutral) sites.
In this central Oregon population of \bgla{}, the mean $\pi_{0}$, was 0.0107 (median:
0.00292, standard deviation: 0.0178).
This is the lowest diversity among the studied categories in the genome, not a
surprising result given the higher functional constraint expected at these sites.
In contrast, mean $\pi_{4}$ was 0.0790 (median: 0.0750, standard deviation: 0.0483),
$\approx$ 1.6x higher than the genome-wide average and the highest in the genome.
The nearly 8-fold difference between $\pi_{0}$ and $\pi_{4}$ highlights the
strong constraint on non-synonymous sites and suggests that purifying selection is
highly effective in this population, consistent with theoretical predictions for
species with large effective population sizes.

\subsection*{\balgla{} exhibits large effective population sizes}

We estimated the effective population size (\Ne) for a central
Oregon \textit{B. glandula} population sample using the whole-genome genotypes.
As a simple estimate of the long-term \Ne{} of this population, we first
calculated \Ne{} using Watterson's estimator of genetic diversity ($\theta_\text{W}$),
observing a mean genome-wide $\theta_\text{W}$ of 0.0617 (median: 0.0635, standard
deviation: 0.0224; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_theta_ne}). Assuming a per-base,
per-generation mutation rate ($\mu$) of $3\times10^{-9}$ (as estimated by
\cite{nunez_footprints_2020} for the acorn barnacle \textit{S. balanoides})
and using the relationship between \Ne{} and $\theta_\text{W}$, this yields a
mean effective size of 5,137,172 (median: 5,289,211, standard deviation: 1,862,683),
which is consistent with the expectation of large \Ne{} in this species.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Additionally, we estimated \Ne{} through time using a coalescent-based
approach with \textsc{msmc2} \citep{dutheil_msmc_2020}. This estimate also placed
the historical effective size of the population on the order of $10^{6}$ individuals
(between $10^{6}$ and $10^{7}$ generations ago) (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_mcmc_ne});
however, given our small sample size and the use of only within sample pairwise
comparisons, we were unable to obtain accurate \Ne{} estimates at more recent
($<10^{5}$ generations) timescales using \textsc{msmc2}.
Nevertheless, even these conservative estimates place \bgla{} among the
largest known effective population sizes for any eukaryote, yet orders of
magnitude below the census sizes observed in the field --- a disparity
central to Lewontin's Paradox and one we return to in the Discussion.
