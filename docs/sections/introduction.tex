\section*{Introduction}

The size of a population is a key factor mediating the mode and tempo of evolution,
particularly as it relates to the strength of stochastic versus deterministic evolutionary
processes \citep{kimura_mutation_1963,gillespie_genetic_2000}. Theory predicts that
selection is less efficacious in small populations \citep{charlesworth_effective_2009,
gravel_when_2016}, resulting in reduced genetic diversity from genetic drift and an
increase in genetic load due to the accumulation of deleterious mutations
\citep{lynch_mutation_1995,lande_risk_1994,dussex_purging_2023}. These dynamics have

without also noting that sub-Sah Africa has higher diversity}
been extensively supported by empirical observations, including distance to sub-Saharan
Africa as a predictor of genetic load in humans \citep{henn_distance_2016} and
the increase in genetic load and inbreeding depression in small populations of
conservation concern \citep{tian_severe_2022,mathur_genetic_2021,
kardos_inbreeding_2023}. In contrast, evolution in large populations is thought to
be primarily mediated by deterministic processes due to an increase in the efficacy
of selection \citep{charlesworth_effective_2009,gravel_when_2016,
lanfear_population_2014}. However, this increased efficacy of selection can itself
reduce genetic diversity through the hitchhiking effect \citep{smith_hitch-hiking_1974}.
In sufficiently large populations, hitchhiking is predicted to be a primary driver
of stochastic diversity loss \citep{gillespie_genetic_2000,gillespie_role_1999}.
In contrast to the well-studied dynamics of small populations, empirical tests of
these mechanisms have been, with a few key exceptions \citep{dey_molecular_2013,
teterina_pervasive_2025}, mostly limited to eukaryotic species with
small-to-moderate historical population sizes \citep{cutter_molecular_2013,
leffler_revisiting_2012,buffalo_quantifying_2021}. This gap highlights the need for
empirical studies in systems with historically large population sizes and elevated
levels of genetic polymorphism.

The Pacific acorn barnacle (\balgla) presents an exceptional model for studying the
dynamics and mechanisms of evolution in large populations. This species is widespread
across the Pacific intertidal of North America, from Baja California to Alaska,
where it can reach densities of tens of thousands of individuals per square meter
\citep{menge_recruitment_2000}. High fecundity and a planktonic larval stage capable
of long-distance dispersal, combined with these enormous census sizes, are consistent
with very large effective population sizes, making \bgla{} an ideal system for
testing theoretical predictions about selection and drift in large populations.

This species has been the focus of extensive ecological and genetic research. Prior work
has linked environmental factors to variation in development, recruitment, dispersal,
and reproduction \citep{berger_spatial_2006,berger_reproduction_2009,
barshis_coastal_2011,pfeiffer-hoyt_modeling_2005,schubart_recruitment_1995}, and
substantial effort has characterized the spatial population structure of \bgla{}
along the Pacific coast, particularly a steep genetic cline on the coast of
California that separates northern and southern populations
\citep{sotka_strong_2004,wares_diversification_2005,wares_maintenance_2019,
wares_diversity_2021}.

\st{These patterns are not unique to \textit{B. glandula}.} Work in other barnacle
species has revealed substantial levels of genetic diversity
\citep{alm_rosenblad_genomic_2021}, as well as complex patterns of selection and
local adaptation at remarkably small spatial scales \citep{nunez_footprints_2020,
nunez_tides_2021}. In the northern acorn barnacle (\textit{Semibalanus balanoides}),
\st{for example}, adaptive alleles in the mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (\textit{Mpi}) gene
are partitioned between low and high intertidal zones less than five meters apart,
with environmental heterogeneity maintaining a balanced polymorphism
\citep{schmidt_environmental_2000,nunez_footprints_2020}. Further work has shown that
different exons of \textit{Mpi} experience distinct selective pressures across spatial
and temporal scales, suggesting a more complex selective landscape than the simple
zonation model implies \citep{nunez_tides_2021}. Despite the depth of this work, it
has focused on a single protein-coding gene, and the genome-wide landscape of genetic
diversity and selection in \textit{S. balanoides} remains unknown.

Pacific acorn barnacle.

We use this assembly to perform whole-genome comparative
analyses, revealing conservation in genome architecture across 165 million years
of barnacle evolution. We further conduct the first genome-wide assessment of
genetic variation in this system, uncovering extreme levels ($>5\%$) of nucleotide
diversity, with over 20 million SNPs observed in just six individuals.
Consistent with theoretical expectations, we observe reduced diversity in
protein-coding sequences, reflecting strong background selection and functional
constraints. McDonald-Kreitman tests further reveal that the majority of
non-synonymous substitutions between species were driven by positive selection,
as expected when selection is highly efficacious in large
populations. Our assembly provides a key resource for future research, laying
the groundwork for \bgla{} as a model for the study of evolution
in large, hyperpolymorphic populations.
