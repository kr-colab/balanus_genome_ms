\begin{abstract}

The size of a population is a key factor mediating the mode and tempo of evolution, particularly as it relates to strength of stochastic versus deterministic evolutionary processes. While the mechanisms underlying the interactions between population size and selection have been studied in population genetics for over a century, empirical knowledge of these dynamics has been limited to species with small-to-moderate historical population sizes. This gap in knowledge highlights the need for empirical studies in systems with historically large population sizes and elevated diversity. The Pacific acorn barnacle (\textit{Balanus glandula}) presents an exceptional model to study evolution in extremely large populations, exhibiting census sizes often exceeding the tens of thousands of individuals per meter squared. We present one of the first large-scale genomic analyses in this system, generating a new chromosome-level genome assembly for this species. This assembly reveals high levels of genetic polymorphism even in a single individual. At a population level, \textit{B. glandula} exhibits extreme levels of polymorphism, with nucleotide diversity surpassing 5\% genome-wide in just a small collection of individuals. Across the genome, nucleotide diversity predictably decreases at functional elements, including both coding and non-coding sequences, likely reflecting strong purifying selection along the genome. These remarkable levels of diversity set \textit{B. glandula} as a unique model for the study of evolution at extreme demographic scales and highlights the importance of testing evolutionary theory across a wide variety of empirical systems.

\end{abstract}

The size of a population is a key factor mediating the mode and tempo of evolution, particularly as it relates to strength of stochastic versus deterministic evolutionary forces. We know from theory that selection is less efficacious in small populations [Kimura1963; Charlesworth2009; Gravel2016], resulting in a reduction in genetic diversity from the stochastic effects of genetic drift and an increase in genetic load due to the accumulations of deleterious mutations [Dussex2023]. These dynamics of evolution in small populations have been extensively supported by empirical observations, e.g., distance to sub-Saharan Africa as a predictor of genetic load in humans [Henn2015], and the increase in genetic load and inbreeding depression in small populations of conservation concern [Tian2022; Mathur2021; Kardos2023]. In contrast, evolution in large populations is thought to be primarily mediated by deterministic processes due to an increase in the efficacy of selection [Charlesworth2009; Gravel2016; Lanfear2014]. Interestingly, this increase in selection can in turn lead to a reduction in genetic diversity due to the hitchhiking of neutral alleles in a stochastic-like fashion [MaynardSmith1974]. In large enough populations, this hitchhiking effect is theorized as the main driver of the stochastic loss of genetic diversity [MaynardSmith1974; Gillespie2000]. Yet, in contrast to small populations, empirical studies of these evolutionary mechanisms has been, with a few key exceptions [Dey2013; Teterina2025], mostly limited to eukaryotic species with small-to-moderate historical population sizes [Cutter2013]. This gap in knowledge highlights the need for empirical studies in systems with historically large population sizes and elevated levels of genetic polymorphism.
\vspace{0.2cm}

The Pacific acorn barnacle (\textit{Balanus glandula}) presents an exceptional model to study the dynamics and mechanism of evolution in large populations. This species is widespread in the intertidal across the Pacific coast of North America, from Baja California to Alaska, where it can reach densities of up to tens of thousands of individuals per square meter [Menge2000]. This species has been the subject of numerous prior studies, with research describing the link between environmental factors and variation in development, recruitment, dispersal, and reproduction [Berger2006; Berger2009; Barshis2011; Nishizaki2014; PfeifferHoyt2005; Schubart1995]. There has also been substantial work regarding the spatial population structure of \textit{B. glandula} along the Pacific coast, particularly with regard to a steep genetic cline in the coast of California that separates populations North and South of this transition point [Sotka2004; Wares2005; Wares2019; Wares2021]. Moreover, \textit{B. glandula} has also been introduced outside its native range, and genetic work has been work to assess invasive populations in Japan [Yorisue2022; Geller2022], Argentina [Geller2022], and South Africa [Simon-Blecher2010].
\vspace{0.2cm}

Previous work on barnacles has shown both substantial levels of genetic diversity [AlmRosenbald2020], as well as complex patterns of selection and local adaptation at small spatial scales [Nunez2020; Nunez2021]. In the northern acorn barnacle (\textit{Semibalanus balanoides}), there is evidence of zonation of adaptive alleles in the mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (\textit{Mpi}) gene between low and high intertidal barnacles less than five meters apart [Nunez2020]. Moreover, while \textit{Mpi} as a whole is under balancing selection across the intertidal, there is evidence for a more complex selection landscape across the gene. Different exons appear to be under varying selection effects across spatial and temporal scales, beyond the low versus high intertidal. While this is a comprehensive look at genetic diversity and selection in a barnacle species, the work primarily focused on a single protein-coding gene. The landscape of genetic diversity and signatures of selection across the \textit{S. balanoides} genome remains unknown.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Similarly, while there has been extensive prior genetic work in \textit{B. glandula}, the species also remains genomically understudied. Previous genetic research in the species has been limited to a handful of nuclear and mitochondrial markers, with the most extensive genetic work conducted using reduced representation sequencing in the absence of a reference genome [Wares2021]. Two reference assemblies for \textit{B. glandula} have been deposited to NCBI's GenBank database (NCBI accessions GCA\_030265075.1 and GCA\_049308085.2); however, they both appear to be highly fragmented and/or incomplete. Most publicly available barnacle genomes follow the same fate, being highly-fragmented likely due to the difficulty of assembling highly polymorphic genomes using short reads. While there are a handful of recent high-quality, chromosome-level assemblies for barnacles [Han2024; Bernot2022; Carlson2025; Blaxter2023], they belong to evolutionary distant species, which limits their use for studies in \textit{B. glandula}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

In this work, we describe a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for the Pacific acorn barnacle. We use this assembly to perform whole-genome comparative analysis, revealing conservation in genome architecture across 165 million years of barnacle evolution. We additionally perform the first assessment of genome-wide genetic variation in this system, revealing extreme levels ($>5\%$) of nucleotide diversity across the genome, with over 20 million SNPs observed in just six individuals. In accordance to theoretical expectations, we observe a reduction in diversity in protein-coding sequences, reflecting strong background selection and functional constraints in barnacle populations. Overall, our new assembly provides a key resource for future research in this system, setting the stage for \textit{B. glandula} as a model for the study of evolution in large, hyperpolymorphic populations.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We assembled a chromosome-level reference genome for the Pacific acorn barnacle using PacBio Hi-Fi long reads and Hi-C chromosome conformation capture. Preliminary assessment of the PacBio Hi-Fi data using k-mers revealed an expected genome size of around 845 Mbp, with a heterozygosity of over 3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_kmers}). Our initial contig-level assembly, generated using \texttt{hifiasm} [Cheng2021; Cheng2022] with default parameters, generated sequences with a total length of 1.64 Gbp, composed of 4,212 fragments, and a contig N50 of 734 kbp. We also assessed gene-completeness using \texttt{BUSCO}s [Simao2015; Manni2021; Tegenfeldt2024], as implemented in \texttt{compleasm} [Huang\&Li2023], resulting in a completeness of 95.66\% (\texttt{S}:45.31\%; \texttt{D}:50.35\%; \texttt{F}:0.79\%; \texttt{M}:3.55\%; \texttt{N}:1,013) for the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} single-copy ortholog set (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). The statistics for this initial assembly, with twice the expected genome size and over 50\% duplicated \texttt{BUSCO}s, suggested the presence of a large fraction of haplotig sequences, reflecting the high heterozygosity observed for the species.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following optimization, both in the integration of additional data types and in the increased stringency of the purging of haplotig and contaminant sequences (see Methods), we generated a contig-level assembly composed of 1,342 sequences, with a total length of 1.05 Gbp, a contig N50 of 1.35 Mbp, and a \texttt{BUSCO} completeness of 93.88\% ([\texttt{S}:89.04\%; \texttt{D}:4.84\%; \texttt{F}:1.18\%; \texttt{M}:4.94\%; \texttt{N}:1,013], \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10}). The contig-level assembly was then scaffolded with Hi-C data using the \texttt{YaHS} software [Zhou2022] (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_hic}). Our scaffolding process generated an assembly with a total length of 1.05 Gbp, composed of 650 scaffolds, a scaffold N50 of 50.96 Mbp, and a \texttt{BUSCO} completeness of 93.98\% ([\texttt{S}:89.24\%; \texttt{D}:4.74\%; \texttt{F}:0.89\%; \texttt{M}:5.13\%; \texttt{N}:1,013], \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10}) (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

This scaffold-level assembly was further processed by performing manual curation of the Hi-C contact map and self-correction using the software \texttt{Inspector} [Chen2021]. This curated assembly was composed of 592 fragments, a total sequence length of 1.04 Gbp, a scaffold N50 of 51.41 Mbp, and a per-base quality value (QV) of 30.49. When assessing completeness, the curated assembly was 94.08\% (\texttt{S}:89.44\%; \texttt{D}:4.64\%; \texttt{F}:0.89\%; \texttt{M}:5.03\%; \texttt{N}:1,013) and 92.77\% (\texttt{S}:87.77\%; \texttt{D}:5.40\%; \texttt{F}:2.93\%; \texttt{M}:4.30\%; \texttt{N}:1,536) gene-complete for the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} \texttt{BUSCO} datasets, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). Of the 1.04 Gbp of total sequence, 906.6 Mbp (86.74\%) was contained in 58 scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp. Moreover, the 16 largest sequences (larger than 8.5 Mbp) contain 841.2 Mbp (80.61\%) of the total sequence, and likely represent 16 putative chromosomes. This karyotype number, N=16, is in agreement with that observed for other species in the genus \textit{Balanus} [Austin1958] and other acorn barnacles in the family Balanidae [Han2024].

\subsection*{Genome annotation}

% Stats and QC on the annotation.

Once the assembly and scaffolding was completed, we annotated and masked repeat sequences in the \textit{B. glandula} reference assembly using the \texttt{EarlGrey} software [Baril2024]. This analysis identified 698.6 Mbp (66.94\%) of the assembly as repetitive elements (Table~\ref{tab:sup_earlgrey_summary}); however, the majority of these, 404.5 Mbp (38.76\% of the assembly), remain unclassified among the major repeat element types. This large proportion of unclassified repeat elements is likely the product of the small number of representative barnacle sequences among curated repeat reference libraries, reflecting barnacle's status as a genomically understudied group. Among the classified repeats, DNA and LINE elements were the two most abundant repeats classes, comprising 8.45\% and 9.16\% of the total assembly, respectively.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following repeat masking of the reference assembly, we annotated protein-coding genes using short-read RNAseq and long-read PacBio Iso-Seq sequencing, combining and curating annotations generated across the two data types (see Methods). This process resulted in 20,234 protein-coding genes annotated in the genome. The resulting annotation was 93.58\% (\texttt{S}:79.76\%; \texttt{D}:13.82\%; \texttt{F}:1.78\%; \texttt{M}:4.64\%; \texttt{N}:1,013) and 84.77\% (\texttt{S}:72.66\%; \texttt{D}:12.11\%; \texttt{F}:4.10\%; \texttt{M}:11.13\%; \texttt{N}:1,536) gene-complete when compared against the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} \texttt{BUSCO} datasets, respectively. Additionally, we compared our annotation against the proteome of four other barnacle species (see Methods) using \texttt{OrthoFinder} [Emms\&Kelly2015; Emms\&Kelly2019], resulting in the assignment of 16,978 (83.91\%) \textit{B. glandula} genes to orthologous groups across barnacles and the identification of 2,576 single-copy orthologs across all five species.
\vspace{0.2cm}

In addition to protein-coding genes, we also annotated non-coding elements in the \textit{B. glandula} reference assembly. We identified 7,532 long, non-coding RNAs (lncRNA), 1,203 transfer RNAs (tRNA), and 24 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. In total, across protein-coding and non-coding transcripts, we were able to annotate 28,993 genes in the genome.

We performed a conserved synteny analysis using the software \texttt{Genespace} [Lovell2022] to identify patterns of large-scale structure across barnacle genomes. We compared our \textit{B. glandula} genome against the chromosome-level genome assemblies of the striped barnacle \textit{Amphibalanus amphitrite} (Balanidae) [Han2024] and the gooseneck barnacle \textit{Pollicipes pollicipes} (Pollicipedidae) [Bernot2022]. These genomes display conservation in their large-scale organization, including one-to-one correspondence between 16 putatively orthologous chromosomes across the three species (Figure~\ref{fig:synteny}). This finding shows that our scaffolding of the \textit{B. glandula} assembly likely reflects the biological structure of the genome and is not the product of technical artifacts, but also suggests the conservation in the genome architecture of barnacle genomes across $\sim$165 Mya of evolution [Han 2024; Bernot2022].

\subsection*{High genetic diversity in the \textit{B. glandula} reference assembly}

We calculated estimates of diversity present in the barnacle genome by genotyping our \textit{B. glandula} reference individual. After filtering low-quality genotypes and masking regions with annotated repeats, we obtained genotypes for 314 million sites along the genome, equivalent to 34.71\% of the 904.94 Mbp present in the 58 scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp (Table~\ref{tab:sup_popgen_stats}). Among these 314 million genotyped sites, 9.08 million (2.89\%) were heterozygous in the reference individual, including 8.73 million SNPs and 356 thousand indels. In total, this variation represent a mean density of 14.66 heterozygous sites per kbp (median: 6; standard deviation: 19.86). Moreover, we observed a total of 399 thousand variant sites within the span of protein-coding sequences, including 144 thousand non-synonymous point mutations, as catergorized by the \texttt{snpEff} software [Cingolani2012]. In this reference individual, the average transcript showed a mean number of 7.74 (median: 3, standard deviation: 14.97) non-synonymous variants, indicating a substantial proportion of coding variation in just a single barnacle individual.

\subsection*{Functional elements are conserved in barnacle genomes}

We used the \texttt{phastCons} software [Siepel2005] on the \texttt{cactus} whole-genome alignment [Armstrong2020] between six barnacle species to identify highly conserved sites in the Pacific acorn barnacle genome. Across the 16 putative chromosomes in the \textit{B. glandula} assembly, \texttt{phastCons} identified 196,141 highly-conserved elements (HCEs), spanning 50.85 Mbp of sequence and 4.92\% of the assembled genome. In \textit{B. glandula}, the HCEs had a mean length of 259.25 bp (median: 42 bp, standard deviation: 990.40 bp).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following the identification of HCEs, we compared their sequence composition against the genome-wide background (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}). For this, we first overlaid the genomic coordinates of the HCEs against the \textit{B. glandula} annotation, obtaining the proportion of annotated features among HCE sites (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}A). Among our target features, including an ``other'' and ``multiple'' class for the unannotated fraction of the genome and annotation overlaps, respectively, all but one is present among HCEs. We do not observe any rRNA sites among our conserved sites; however, this is likely the product of the whole-genome alignment, given rRNA's repetitive nature and alignment difficulty [e.g., Gillispie2004], and not an accurate measure of conservation. Aside from the absence of rRNAs, the sequence composition of HCE largely follows the composition of the whole genome. Most annotated features do not appear substantially enriched among HCE sites (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}B, Table~\ref{tab:sup_phastcons_enrichment}). The two clear exceptions are coding sequences (CDSs) and tRNAs. CDSs display an average two-fold enrichment among HCEs (mean: 1.96, median: 1.98, standard deviation: 0.846), reflecting the conservation of protein-coding genes among barnacle genomes. Similarly, tRNAs are five-fold enriched in HCEs (mean: 4.99, median: 4.10, standard deviation:), the likely product of the strong functional constraint expected for these sequences.

\subsection*{Patterns of molecular evolution in protein-coding genes}

To look at the rate of molecular evolution in protein coding sequences, we calculated at the pairwise rate of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S}) between \textit{B. glandula} and its congener, the wrinkled barnacle \textit{Balanus crenatus}. We identified and aligned 9,082 single-copy orthologs between both species. The mean pairwise d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} was 0.206 (median: 0.158, standard deviation: 0.191; Fig~\ref{fig:sup_dnds}). This ratio indicates that, on average, protein coding sequencing in these barnacle genomes are under purifying selection, reflecting the expected evolutionary constraints over sequences of functional importance.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Of the 9 thousand genes assessed, 70 (0.77\%) displayed a d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} $>$ 1, and are likely candidates under positive selection. The predicted function of the majority ($>80\%$) of these genes is unknown, as determined by the functional annotation generated by the \texttt{EnTap} software [Hart2019]. This is expected, since homology is likely harder to determine for fast-evolving sequences putatively under strong selection Regardless, we found several genes with known function in our candidates under positive selection. Among then, we found a follistatin-A-like homolog, known to be involved to myogenesis and muscle development in crustaceans [Yan2021]. We also observe a homolog of the gamma-interferon-inducible lysosomal thiol reductase, known to be involved in innate immune response in crustaceans [Lui2019]. Additionally, we find homologs to tumor protein p53-inducible protein 11 (which is involved in cell cycle regulation), chymotrypsin-like protease CTRL-1, and the protein quiver gene.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We expanded on our d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} measures of positive selection in protein-coding sequences in the \textit{B. glandula} genome by incorporating population-level polymorphism using the McDonald-Kreitman test [McDonald\&Kreitman1991]. To achieve this, we incorporated genetic variants observed in a \textit{B. glandula} population by taking genetic variants present across the whole-genome of seven individuals (including our reference individual and six additional samples, see below), and comparing those against the orthologous coding sequences in the \textit{B. crenatus} outgroup.
\vspace{0.2cm}

After filtering alignments both by length ($<$100 bp) and by the presence of premature stop codons and frameshift mutations, we calculated the McDonald-Kreitman tests for 7,702 orthologs sequences between \textit{B. glandula} and \textit{B. crenatus}. Eight-hundred and thirty-nine (839, 10.89\%) of these orthologs showed a significant deviation from the expected proportion of non-synonymous and synonymous substitutions and polymorphisms, following \textit{p}-value correction (Fig~\ref{fig:mkTest}). We additionally calculated the Neutrality Index (NI) [Rand\&Kann1996] at each ortholog. We observe a mean NI value of 0.736 (median: 0.493, standard deviation: 1.518), suggesting that, on average, protein coding sequences in \textit{B. glandula} showed an excess of non-synonymous substitutions and are evolving under positive directional selection when compared to the outgroup sequence. Additionally, we calculated the average genome-wide $\alpha$, a measure of the proportion of non-synonymous substitutions fixed by positive selection [Smith\&Eyre-Walker2002; Stoletzki\&Eyre-Walker], finding that 47.49\% of non-synonymous substitutions in this \textit{B. glandula} population were fixed as a product of positive selection.

\subsection*{Barnacle populations exhibit elevated genetic polymorphism}

We sequenced the whole genomes of six \textit{B. glandula} individuals collected from Cape Perpetua, in the central Oregon coast. After a stringent filtering of sites with low coverage, low quality, and/or missing genotypes, we retained a total of 111.6 million genotyped sites (Table~\ref{tab:sup_popgen_stats}), 21.4 million (19.16\%) of which were variant in the sequenced individuals. Among these variant sites, we recovered 20.5 million point mutations, including 996 thousand SNPs that were multiallelic in this set of just six individuals. In total, the amount of polymorphism observed in our sampling translates to a mean density of 41.05 variant sites per kbp (median: 29, standard deviation: 40.82). Moreover, 1.12 million of these variant sites (5.23\%) were located within the span of coding sequences. These coding variants included 360 thousand missense (non-synonymous) mutations, corresponding to an average (mean) of 18.93 non-synonymous variants per transcript (median: 10, standard deviation: 28.50). Overall, these represent a substantial proportion of coding variation segregating in this central Oregon population.
\vspace{0.2cm}

This \textit{B. glandula} population displayed a mean genome-wide nucleotide diversity (\textpi{}) of 0.0506 (median: 0.0531, standard deviation: 0.0188; Fig~\ref{fig:genome_pi}; Table~\ref{tab:sup_feature_pi}). Along each chromosome, we did not observe any strong correlation between the density of specific genomic features (e.g., CDSs, lncRNAs, tRNAs, introns, and repeats) and \textpi{}, particularly not at the scale of 10 kbp (Fig~\ref{fig:genome_pi}; Fig~\ref{fig:sup_pi_pairplot}). However, stronger relationships are likely present at smaller genomic intervals (see below). The strongest correlation was, expectedly, observed between the density of segregating sites along 10 kbp windows and \textpi{} (Spearman's $\rho$: 0.706; Fig~\ref{fig:sup_pi_pairplot}). The proportion of segregating sites in itself was not correlated with the density of particular genomic features, except for repeats; however, this is likely an artifact of the masking of genotypes located within repetitive intervals in the reference (see Methods).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Looking at diversity across the genome, we observe an overall reduction in \textpi{} at putatively functional sites. For example, the mean \textpi{} in sequences spanning annotated CDSs is 0.0302 (median: 0.0270, standard deviation: 0.0221; Fig~\ref{fig:feature_pi}; Fig~\ref{fig:sup_pi_hist}; Table~\ref{tab:sup_feature_pi}), which represents a $\sim$ 1.6x reduction in diversity when compared to the genome-wide average. A reduction in diversity of similar magnitude is also observed in the sequences of lncRNAs (1.4x) and 5' UTRs (1.3x). The largest reduction in diversity when compared to the genome-wide background was observed in tRNAs, with \textpi{} $\sim$ 4.3x lower than the genomic background and in agreement with their high functional constrained as observed from their enrichment in highly-conserved elements in the genome. In contrast to most other elements of the genome, introns display a relative increase in diversity, with a mean \textpi{} of 0.0646 (median: 0.0650, standard deviation: 0.0299), $\sim$ 1.3x higher than the genome-wide average.

Zooming in further on coding sequences, we further explored the patterns of \textpi{} at 0-fold and 4-fold degenerate sites, effectively comparing diversity at constrained versus neutral (or putatively neutral) sites. \subsection*{\textit{B. glandula} exhibits large effective population sizes}

We estimated the effective population size (N\textsubscript{e}) of this central Oregon \textit{B. glandula} population using the whole-genome genotypes. First, we calculated N\textsubscript{e} using the Watterson's estimator of genetic diversity ($\theta_W$), observing a genome-wide mean $\theta_W$ of 0.0617 (median: 0.0635, standard deviation: 0.0224; Fig~\ref{fig:sup_theta_ne}). Using the relationship between the effective population size and the Watterson's estimator, $\theta_W=4N_{e}\mu$, we calculated the mean effective size of this population as 5,137,172 (median: 5,289,211, standard deviation: 1,862,683), according to a mutation rate per-base, per-generation ($\mu$) of $3\times10^{-9}$ (as estimated by [Nunez2020] for the acorn barnacle \textit{S. balanoides}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Additionally, we did a coalescent-based estimation of N\textsubscript{e} through time using \texttt{msmc2}. This estimate also placed the historical (between $10^{6}$ and $10^{7}$ generations ago) effective size of the population in the magnitude of $10^{6}$ effective individuals (Fig~\ref{fig:sup_mcmc_ne}); however, with our small sampling and estimating using only pairs of haplotypes per-individual, we were unable to obtain accurate N\textsubscript{e} estimates at more recent ($<10^{5}$) estimates.

\subsection*{Genome assembly in highly polymorphic species}

% The idea here is to say that we have all these improvement in assembly and data analysis for genomes. These are all good, but not really applicable for these very diverse system. Just highlighting the gap in knowledge and the need for improvements in the field. Good hook for cyclothone and other projects.

Improvements in the affordability, accessibility, and accuracy in sequencing technologies has increased the generation of high quality reference genomes across the tree of life. Over the last decade, we have seen the research outcomes of large genome assembly consortiums, e.g., the Vertebrate Genome Project [Rhie2021] and the Earth BioGenome Project [Blaxter2025], tackling the production of high quality genomes across diverse taxa. We have also seen the generation of complete genomes, sequencing chromosomes at a telomore-to-telomere scale [Nurk2022; Yoo2025], as well as the sequencing and assembly of some of the largest and most repetitive genomes known in nature [Campos-Dominguez2025]. Alongside these advances, there has also been access to cutting-edge best practice protocols for the application of these analyses to new taxa in a reproducible fashion [e.g., Lawniczak2025; Li\&Durbin2025].
\vspace{0.2cm}

Despite these advances, there appears to be a gap in knowledge in the analysis of highly polymorphic organisms, such as barnacles. High heterozygosity presents problems during the assembly process, e.g., hindering the differentiation between paralogs and haplotypes, which often results in the inflation of the genome size and a high proportion of duplicates sequences [Guiglielmoni2024; Asalone2020; Mochizuki2023; Roach2018]. The most common approach is to manage these haplotypes \textit{a posteriori}, purging them after initial assembly [Guan2020; Roach2018]; however, while this approach can be successful in generating haploid assemblies, as we have shown here, it is not without its drawbacks. First, purging haplotypes could lead to a reduction in the gene completeness in the main assembly, as primary haplotypes might be incorrectly labelled as haplotigs, leading to their removal. Secondly, the ability to identify and remove haplotypes is dependent on the properties of the assembly. Sequences that are too fragmented and/or two divergent from one another are likely harder to purge due to difficulties in mapping. Moreover, when performing phased diploid assemblies, the excessive presence of haplotypes will interfere with phasing, leading to a main haploid assembly that is a mosaic between the two haplotypes and/or the poor quality of the secondary assembly.
\vspace{0.2cm}

These difficulties drive the need for the development of methods and analysis best practices focused on the assembly of highly heterozygous genomes, and for a further exploration of how elevated heterozygosity affects \textit{de novo} assembly parameters. While some improvements have been made in recent years, e.g., in \texttt{hifiasm} with the direct purging of haplotigs during assembly and the integration of Hi-C data to phase haplotypes without the need of parental genomes [Cheng2022], further work is likely needed before we can obtain haplotype-resolved genome assemblies for hyper polymorphic organisms.

\subsection*{The magnitude of genetic diversity in barnacle populations}

Our work here shows that a Pacific acorn barnacle population from the central Oregon coast exhibits genetic diversity of over 5\% (mean genome-wide \textpi{} of 0.0506). These levels of genetic diversity are higher than those observed for other model systems known for their relatively large population sizes. For example, the genome-wide nucleotide diversity we observe here in barnacles is tenfold higher than that observed in large-scale surveys in \textit{Drosophila} [Nunez2025; Li2025]. Even the diversity observed at zero-fold degenerate sites, our least diverse in the genome as the likely product of functional constraint, is still an order of magnitude higher than the diverse observed in these diverse \textit{Drosophila} populations.

\subsection*{Barnacles as models for evolution at biological extremes}

% Now, bringing the hyperpolymorphic stuff. We are above the "cutfoff" of hyperpolymorphic. But also, the Hart2025 "limit" of 1% for species boundaries. We are past that here.

The magnitude of diversity observed in barnacles solidly places them among hyper-polymorphic populations, those who exhibit diversity above 5\% [Cutter2013]. This adds barnacles to growing list of emerging hyperpolymorphic models systems, which are key to studying the limits of biological variation and molecular in natural populations. Moreover, our finding of diversity also has important implications for the study of species boundaries. For example, recent work by [Hart2025] denotes how levels of above 1\% neutral diversity in Eukaryotes likely serve as biological limits for the definition of species. Barnacles and other hyperpolymorphic populations puts these putative cutoffs into question. We observe levels of genome-wide diversity of 5\% in just a very small portion of the biological range of the Pacific acorn barnacle. Given the species' broader geographical and ecological distribution, genetic diversity is likely to increase when looking across the whole range. This observation reiterates the importance of future studies of genetic diversity of this species over a broader geographic range, providing some insights on the mechanisms that mantain population and species boundaries in the presence of extreme genetic diversity.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Moreover, barnacles appear to be an emerging model for the study of the dynamics between effective and census population sizes. From our results on diversity, we observe that this barnacle population has an effective size approximately 10\textsuperscript{6}. However, these populations also exhibit densities of 10\textsuperscript{4} individuals per m\textsuperscript{2} [Menge2000]. Based on this estimate, the census size of the Pacific barnacle population in central Oregon should be in the billions (if not more) of barnacles. While the general disparity between these two population size estimates has been long known, i.e., Lewontin's Paradox [Lewontin1974], the mechanisms underlying the differences have been long debated [Gillespie1999; Gillespie2000; MaynardSmith1974; Kimura1983] and are still the subjects of study [Buffalo2021]. Our work on the Pacific acorn barnacle provides an opportunity to study these process empirically.

\section*{Conclusion}

In this work, we describe the first high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for the Pacific acorn barnacle, \textit{Balanus glandula}. Using this new assembly, we first identify conserved synteny among barnacle genomes despite 165 Mya of divergence. Functional elements are highly conserved in the genome of the species; however, patterns of molecular evolution highlight the precense of positive selection as a key driver in the divergence of coding sequences between closely related barnacle species. At a population level, Oregon populations of \textit{B. glanula} appear to be hyperpolymorphic, showing levels of genetic diversity of over 5\% along the genome. Yet, we find preliminary evidence of background selection over protein-coding and non-coding genes, reiterating the functional constraint genes. Overall, our work highlights the role of the Pacific acorn barnacle as an emerging model for the study of genetic variation and the mechanisms of evolution at biological extremes.

\subsection*{Genome assembly}

\subsubsection*{Genome size and heterozygosity estimation}

We estimated the genome size and heterozygosity of the \textit{B. glandula} genome using k-mers. First, we used the \texttt{count} command of the \texttt{jellyfish} software version \texttt{2.2.10} [Marcais2011] to count the unique k-mers present in the PacBio HiFi reads. We counted k-mers of size 21 (\texttt{--mer-len 21}) present in both strands (\texttt{--canonical}). From these counts, we then generated an empirical k-mer distribution using the \texttt{jellyfish histo} command. Finally, we used the software \texttt{GenomeScope2} version \texttt{2.0} [Ranallo-Benavidez2020] to model the distribution of k-mers in the diploid genome (\texttt{--ploidy 2}) based on the empirical counts.

\subsubsection*{Base contig-level genome assembly}

A base, contig-level genome assembly for \textit{B. glandula} was generated from the PacBio HiFi reads using \texttt{hifiasm} version \texttt{0.19.6-r595} [Cheng2021; Cheng2022] with default parameters. This base assembly was assesed for contiguity using \texttt{quast} version \texttt{5.2.0} [Gurevich2013], and for gene-completeness with \texttt{compleasm} version \texttt{0.12-r237} [Huang\&Li2023] using the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} reference \texttt{BUSCO} dataset [Manni2021; Tegenfeldt2024].

\subsubsection*{Assembly decontamination}

Following the generation of a base, contig-level genome assembly, we detected endogenous contamination (i.e., sequences not belonging to \textit{B. glandula}) in both reads and assembled contigs using the \texttt{blobtoolkit} software version \texttt{4.3.0} [Challis2020]. To achieve this, we first created a new \texttt{blobtools} databse, using the \texttt{create} command, specifying the contig-level assembly as our reference sequence (\texttt{--fasta}), the NCBI taxonomical identifier for \textit{B. glandula} (\texttt{--taxid 110520}), and the NCBI reference taxonomy files (\texttt{--taxdump}, downloaded on 2023/12/13).
\vspace{0.2cm}

To add depth of coverage to this database, we aligned our PacBio HiFi sequencing reads to our base, contig-level reference assembly using \texttt{minimap2} version \texttt{2.26-r1175} [Li2018], running in the \texttt{map-hifi} configuration (\texttt{-x map-hifi}) and exporting alignments in SAM format (\texttt{-a}). Alignments were then processed, sorted, and indexed using \texttt{samtools} version \texttt{1.18} [Li2009]. The processed alignments were then added to the \texttt{blobtools} database using the \texttt{add} command.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We did a taxonomical assignment of each sequence in the assembly by first aligning our contigs against NCBI's non-redundant database (downloaded on 2023/12/13). We aligned each contig against the database using \texttt{blastn} version \texttt{2.15.0+}, as implemented in \texttt{BLAST+} [Camacho2009], specifying a minimum e-value cutoff (\texttt{-evalue}) of $1\times10^{-10}$, a maximum of 25 target sequence exported in the output (\texttt{-max\_target\_seqs 25}), and exporting in tabular format (\texttt{-outfmt 6}). These BLAST allginments were then added to the \texttt{blobtools} database using the \texttt{add} command, specifying the rule for taxonomical assignment (\texttt{--taxrule bestsumorder}). Using this approach, the \texttt{blobtools} database provides a single taxonomic assignment to each assembled contig.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following taxonomical assignment of the assembled contigs, we used the \texttt{blobtools filter} command to obtain the IDs of sequencings reads that were assigned to a given taxon. We specified paths of our reference contigs (\texttt{--fasta}), alignments (\texttt{--cov}), and sequencing reads (\texttt{--fastq}). For filtering, we specified the parameters to select the reads aligned to contigs assigned to the class Thecostraca (\texttt{--Keys=Thecostraca}) using a given taxonomic assignment rule (\texttt{--param bestsumorder\_class}). We then specified the (\texttt{--invert}) option to specify that we wished to export the IDs of reads matching this assignment (i.e., keeping only reads specific to the barnacle class Thecostraca).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Lastly, we used the IDs of the Thecostraca-assigned reads to filter our raw PacBio HiFi FASTQ file using the \texttt{subseq} command from the \texttt{seqtk} software version \texttt{1.4-r130} (\url{https://github.com/lh3/seqtk}). This filtering in 5.74 million Thecostraca-specific PacBio HiFi reads, representing 96.8\% of the total sequencing library. This taxonomic assignment and read filtering was also performed on the ONT reads, retaining 371 thousand (95.8\%) reads.

\subsubsection*{Optimized contig-level genome assembly}

We generated a new contig-level genome assembly using the Thecostraca-specific data and iterating over different assembly parameters and filtering schemes, selecting an optimal assembly based on the best contiguity and gene-completeness metrics, as determined by \texttt{quast} and \texttt{compleasm}, respectively. This optimized assembly was generated using \texttt{hifiasm} version \texttt{0.19.8-r603}, including our Thecostraca-specific PacBio HiFi reads as well as the ultra-long ONT (\texttt{--ul}) and Hi-C reads (\texttt{--h1}, \texttt{--h2}). This assembly was done using using an estimated haploid genome size of 800 Mbp (\texttt{--hg-size 800m}), dropping k-mers observed over ten times the average coverage (\texttt{-D 10.0}), and allowing scaffolding (\texttt{--dual-scaf}). For identifying and correctly phasing haplotype sequences, we set the expected coverage at homozygous sites (\texttt{--hom-cov 68}) and the upper bound of coverage to purging duplicates (\texttt{--purge-max 68}) at 68x, along with setting the similarity threshold between duplicate haplotigs to 25\% (\texttt{-s 0.25}). This assembly was composed of 2,218 contigs, with a total length of 1.29 Gbp, and a contig N50 of 1.16 Mbp.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following the initial assembly of contigs, we purged haplotig sequences using the \texttt{purge\_dups} software version \texttt{1.2.5} [Guan2020]. Following the \texttt{purge\_dups} documentation, we first mapped the PacBio HiFi reads to this contig-level assembly using \texttt{minimap2 -x map-hifi}. We then calculated the read-depth histogram using the \texttt{pbcstat} command, following by determining the base-level coverage cutoffs using the command \texttt{calcuts}. Subsequently, we split assembly at the Ns, if present, using the \texttt{split\_fa} command, followed by a self-alignment using \texttt{minimap2 -x asm5}. The \texttt{purge\_dups} command was then used to mark haplotig sequences in a BED file according to the coverage cutoffs empirically determined from the data. Lastly, the assembly FASTA was processed to remove sequences tagged as haplotigs using the \texttt{get\_seqs} command, only removing sequences at the ends of contigs \texttt{-e} and allowing the splitting of contigs \texttt{-s}. The purging process removed 876 fragments from the assembly, accounting for 240 Mbp of haplotig sequences (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).

\subsubsection*{Hi-C Scaffolding}

We scaffolded the purged contig-level assembly into chromosome-level scaffolds using Hi-C data. First, we aligned the Hi-C short-read data to our assembly using \texttt{bwa mem} version \texttt{0.7.17-r1188} [Li2013], setting the minimum alignment score to output to zero (\texttt{-T 0}), skipping make rescue (\texttt{-S}), mate pairing (\texttt{-P}), and setting the smallest query coordinate as primary for split alignments (\texttt{-5}). Alignments were converted to BAM format using \texttt{samtools} version \texttt{1.18}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We processed the aligned Hi-C data using the \texttt{pairtools} version \texttt{1.0.2} software [Open2C2023], following the Dovetail analysis pipeline (\url{https://dovetail-analysis.readthedocs.io}). Briefly, we recorded valid ligation events using \texttt{pairtools parse}, setting a minimum mapping quality of 40 (\texttt{--min-mapq 40}), a maximum inter-alignment gap of 30 kbp (\texttt{--max-inter-align-gap 30}), and reporting the 5'-most unique alignment when detecting multiple ligation events (\texttt{--walks-policy 5unique}). We then sorted the processed alignment pairs using \texttt{pairtools sort}, and marked PCR duplicate reads using \texttt{pairtools dedup --mark-dups}. Finally, we used \texttt{pairtools split} to process the data into a \texttt{BAM} file of valid alignments and a \texttt{pairs} file with the read pairs forming the contact map. The resulting alignment \texttt{BAM} was sorted and indexed using \texttt{samtools}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

The processed Hi-C alignments were used to scaffold the genome using the \texttt{YaHS} software version \texttt{1.2a.1} [Zhou2022]. Since our assembly was in highly fragmented contigs, we reduced the minimum resolution size to 1 kbp (\texttt{-r 1000}). Following the \texttt{YaHS} documentation, we generated a Hi-C contact map using the \texttt{juicer} suite of tools [Durand2016; Dudchenko2018]. First, we used \texttt{juicer} version \texttt{1.1} \texttt{pre} command to convert the \texttt{YaHS} output into the \texttt{juicer} format in assembly mode (\texttt{-a}). We then ran \texttt{juicer tools} version \texttt{1.9.9} to generate the \texttt{*.assembly} and \texttt{*.hic} files required for manual curation using the \texttt{Juicebox} GUI version \texttt{2.17.00}. Following manual curation of the contact map, we regenerated the assembly using \texttt{juicer post}. This scaffolded assembly was composed of 650 fragments, a total length of 1.05 Gbp, and a scaffold N50 of 50.96 Mbp (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).

\subsubsection*{Polishing and correcting}

After manual curation of the scaffolded genome, we performed one round of polishing and automatic curation using the \texttt{Inspector} software version \texttt{1.0.1} [Chen2021]. First, we evaluated the assembly using \texttt{inspector.py}, mapping the PacBio HiFi reads against the assembly. The assembly was then corrected using \texttt{inspector-correct.py}.

\subsection*{Genome annotation}

\subsubsection*{Repeat annotation}

We identified and classified repetitive elements in the \textit{B. glandula} genome using the \texttt{EarlGrey} software version \texttt{4.1.0} [Baril2024]. Before running \texttt{EarlGrey}, we first downloaded the \texttt{Dfam 3.8} database [Storer2021], extracting the annotated arthropod repeat sequences to generate an initial consensus masking library. We provided this initial consensus library to \texttt{EarlGrey} (\texttt{-l}). We also used ``arthropoda'' as the search term for \texttt{RepeatMasker} version \texttt{4.1.2} [Smit2013]. In addition, we clustered the final TE library to reduce redundancy (\texttt{-c yes}) and generated a soft-masked genome (\texttt{-d yes}).

\subsubsection*{Annotation of protein-coding genes}

After masking the repetitive sequences in the genome, we took an integrative approach to annotate protein-coding genes, combining evidence from both short-read RNAseq data,PacBio IsoSeq long reads, and protein sequences. For protein-level evidence, we downloaded the Arthropoda and Crustacea partitions of \texttt{OrthoDB} version \texttt{11} [Kriventseva2019; Zdobnov2020].
\vspace{0.2cm}

When processing the short-read data, we first filtered and processed the raw RNAseq Illumina reads using \texttt{fastp} version \texttt{0.23.4} [Chen2018; Chen2023], setting the minimum length of reads post-filtering to 25 bp (\texttt{--length\_required 25}) and enabling the detection of adapter sequences in the paired reads (\texttt{--detect\_adapter\_for\_pe}). We then aligned the processed RNAseq reads to the \textit{B. glandula} assembly using \texttt{HISAT2} version \texttt{2.2.1} [Kim2019], exporting alignments tailored for \textit{de novo} transcriptome assemblers (\texttt{--dta}). The alignments were further processed and sorted using \texttt{samtools}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Using this aligned RNAseq data, we annotated protein-coding genes using \texttt{BRAKER} version \texttt{3.0.8} [Gabriel 2024]. We ran \texttt{BRAKER} in \texttt{ETP} mode [Brůna2024], providing both RNAseq alignments (\texttt{--bams}) and Arthropod protein sequences (\texttt{--prot\_seq}) as evidence. We also used the Arthropod dataset from \texttt{BUSCO} [Simao2015] to enforce this set of orthologs as hints for \texttt{Augustus} and reduce the number of missing single-copy orthologs in the final annotation (\texttt{--busco\_lineage arthropoda\_odb10}). After running \texttt{BUSCO}, we assessed the quality of the generated annotation using \texttt{compleasm} version \texttt{0.2.5} [Huang2023] in \text{protein} mode, using the Arthropod ortholog set as reference (\texttt{--lineage arthropoda\_odb10}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

In parallel, we annotated protein-coding genes using the PacBio IsoSeq data, aligning it to the assembly using \texttt{minimap2} version \texttt{2.26-r1175} (\texttt{-x splice:hq}) and processing the downstream alignments with \texttt{samtools}. We then annotated the genome using the long-read branch of \texttt{BRAKER} version \texttt{3.0.8}. Similar to the short-read annotation, we ran \texttt{BRAKER} in \texttt{ETP} mode, using the aligned IsoSeq data (\texttt{--bam}) and Arthropod protein sequences (\texttt{--prot\_seq}) as evidence, and enforcing the \texttt{BUSCO} Arthropod reference set in the annotation (\texttt{--busco\_lineage arthropoda\_odb10}). Lastly, we used \texttt{compleasm protein} to assess the completeness of the annotation.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Once the RNAseq- and IsoSeq-based \texttt{BRAKER} annotations were completed, we merged the two using \texttt{TSEBRA}. As recommended by published protocols [Bruna2025], we merged the two annotations by providing the hint files (\texttt{--hintfiles}) and final GTF file (\texttt{--gtf}) of each individual \texttt{BRAKER} run as inputs to the \texttt{tsebra.py} script. We assessed the gene-completeness of the merged annotation using \texttt{compleasm protein}, using both the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} reference datasets.
