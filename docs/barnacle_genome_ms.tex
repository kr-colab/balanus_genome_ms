\begin{abstract}

The size of a population is a key factor mediating the mode and tempo of evolution, 
particularly as it relates to strength of stochastic versus deterministic evolutionary 
processes. While the mechanisms underlying the interactions between population size 
and selection have been studied in population genetics for over a century, empirical 
knowledge of these dynamics has been limited to species with small-to-moderate 
historical population sizes. This gap in knowledge highlights the need for empirical 
studies in systems with historically large population sizes and elevated diversity. 
The Pacific acorn barnacle (\textit{Balanus glandula}) presents an exceptional model 
to study evolution in extremely large populations, exhibiting census sizes often 
exceeding the tens of thousands of individuals per meter squared. We present one 
of the first large-scale genomic analyses in this system, generating a new 
chromosome-level genome assembly for this species. This assembly reveals high levels 
of genetic polymorphism even in a single individual. At a population level, 
\textit{B. glandula} exhibits extreme levels of polymorphism, with nucleotide 
diversity surpassing 5\% genome-wide in just a small collection of individuals. 
Across the genome, nucleotide diversity predictably decreases at functional elements, 
including both coding and non-coding sequences, likely reflecting strong purifying 
selection along the genome. These remarkable levels of diversity set \textit{B. 
glandula} as a unique model for the study of evolution at extreme demographic scales and 
highlights the importance of testing evolutionary theory across a wide variety of 
empirical systems.

\end{abstract}

The size of a population is a key factor mediating the mode and tempo of evolution, 
particularly as it relates to the strength of stochastic versus deterministic evolutionary
forces. We know from theory that selection is less efficacious in small populations 
(\cite{kimura_mutation_1963,charlesworth_effective_2009,gravel_when_2016}), resulting 
in a reduction in genetic diversity from the stochastic effects of genetic drift and 
an increase in genetic load due to the accumulation of deleterious mutations 
(\cite{dussex_purging_2023}). These dynamics of evolution in small populations have 
been extensively supported by empirical observations, e.g., distance to sub-Saharan 
Africa as a predictor of genetic load in humans (\cite{henn_distance_2016}), and 
the increase in genetic load and inbreeding depression in small populations of 
conservation concern (\cite{tian_severe_2022,mathur_genetic_2021,kardos_inbreeding_2023}). 
In contrast, evolution in large populations is thought to be primarily mediated by 
deterministic processes due to an increase in the efficacy of selection 
(\cite{charlesworth_effective_2009,gravel_when_2016,lanfear_population_2014}). 
Interestingly, this increase in selection can in turn lead to a reduction in genetic 
diversity due to the hitchhiking of neutral alleles in a stochastic-like fashion 
(\cite{smith_hitch-hiking_1974}). In large enough populations, this hitchhiking 
effect is theorized as the main driver of the stochastic loss of genetic diversity 
(\cite{smith_hitch-hiking_1974,gillespie_genetic_2000,gillespie_role_1999}). 
Yet, in contrast to small populations, empirical studies of these evolutionary
mechanisms have been, with a few key exceptions (\cite{dey_molecular_2013,
teterina_pervasive_2025}), mostly limited to eukaryotic species with 
small-to-moderate historical population sizes (\cite{cutter_molecular_2013}). 
This gap in knowledge highlights the need for empirical studies in systems 
with historically large population sizes and elevated levels of genetic polymorphism.
\vspace{0.2cm}

The Pacific acorn barnacle (\textit{Balanus glandula}) presents an exceptional 
model to study the dynamics and mechanisms of evolution in large populations. 
This species is widespread in the intertidal across the Pacific coast of North 
America, from Baja California to Alaska, where it can reach densities of up to 
tens of thousands of individuals per square meter (\cite{menge_recruitment_2000}). 
This species has been the subject of numerous prior studies, with research 
describing the link between environmental factors and variation in development, 
recruitment, dispersal, and reproduction (\cite{berger_spatial_2006,
berger_reproduction_2009,barshis_coastal_2011,pfeiffer-hoyt_modeling_2005,
schubart_recruitment_1995}). There has also been substantial work regarding 
the spatial population structure of \textit{B. glandula} along the Pacific coast, 
particularly with regard to a steep genetic cline on the coast of California 
that separates populations North and South of this transition point 
(\cite{sotka_strong_2004,wares_diversification_2005,wares_maintenance_2019,
wares_diversity_2021}). Moreover, \textit{B. glandula} has also been 
introduced outside its native range, and genetic work has been done to assess 
invasive populations in Japan (\cite{yorisue_lack_2022,geller_sources_2008}), 
Argentina (\cite{geller_sources_2008}), and South Africa 
(\cite{simon-blecher_balanus_2008}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Previous work on barnacles has shown both substantial levels of genetic diversity 
(\cite{alm_rosenblad_genomic_2021}), as well as complex patterns of selection and 
local adaptation at small spatial scales (\cite{nunez_footprints_2020,nunez_tides_2021}). 
In the northern acorn barnacle (\textit{Semibalanus balanoides}), there is 
evidence of zonation of adaptive alleles in the mannose-6-phosphate isomerase 
(\textit{Mpi}) gene between low and high intertidal barnacles less than five 
meters apart (\cite{nunez_footprints_2020}). Moreover, while \textit{Mpi} as a 
whole is under balancing selection across the intertidal, there is evidence for 
a more complex selection landscape across the gene. Different exons appear to be 
under varying selection effects across spatial and temporal scales, beyond the 
low versus high intertidal. While this is a comprehensive look at genetic diversity 
and selection in a barnacle species, the work primarily focused on a single 
protein-coding gene. The landscape of genetic diversity and signatures of selection 
across the \textit{S. balanoides} genome remains unknown.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Similarly, while there has been extensive prior genetic work in \textit{B. glandula}, 
the species also remains genomically understudied. Previous genetic research in the 
species has been limited to a handful of nuclear and mitochondrial markers, with the 
most extensive genetic work conducted using reduced representation sequencing in the 
absence of a reference genome (\cite{wares_diversity_2021}). Two reference assemblies 
for \textit{B. glandula} have been deposited to NCBI's GenBank database (NCBI 
accessions GCA\_030265075.1 and GCA\_049308085.2); however, they both appear to be 
highly fragmented and/or incomplete. Most publicly available barnacle genomes follow 
the same fate, being highly-fragmented likely due to the difficulty of assembling 
highly polymorphic genomes using short reads. While there are a handful of recent 
high-quality, chromosome-level assemblies for barnacles (\cite{han_new_2024,
bernot_chromosome-level_2022,carlson_reference_2025,blaxter_genome_2023}), they 
belong to evolutionarily distant species, which limits their use for studies in 
\textit{B. glandula}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

In this work, we describe a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for the 
Pacific acorn barnacle. We use this assembly to perform whole-genome comparative 
analysis, revealing conservation in genome architecture across 165 million years 
of barnacle evolution. We additionally perform the first assessment of genome-wide 
genetic variation in this system, revealing extreme levels ($>5\%$) of nucleotide 
diversity across the genome, with over 20 million SNPs observed in just six individuals. 
In accordance with theoretical expectations, we observe a reduction in diversity in 
protein-coding sequences, reflecting strong background selection and functional 
constraints in barnacle populations. Overall, our new assembly provides a key 
resource for future research in this system, setting the stage for \textit{B. 
glandula} as a model for the study of evolution in large, hyperpolymorphic 
populations.
\vspace{0.2cm}

\subsection*{Genome assembly}

We assembled a chromosome-level reference genome for the Pacific acorn barnacle 
using PacBio Hi-Fi long reads and Hi-C chromosome conformation capture. Preliminary 
assessment of the PacBio Hi-Fi data using k-mers revealed an expected genome size 
of around 845 Mbp, with a heterozygosity of over 3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_kmers}). 
Our initial contig-level assembly, generated using \texttt{hifiasm} 
(\cite{cheng_haplotype-resolved_2021,cheng_haplotype-resolved_2022}) with 
default parameters, generated sequences with a total length of 1.64 Gbp, 
composed of 4,212 fragments, and a contig N50 of 734 kbp. We also assessed 
gene-completeness using \texttt{BUSCO}s (\cite{simao_busco_2015,manni_busco_2021,
tegenfeldt_orthodb_2025}), as implemented in \texttt{compleasm} 
(\cite{huang_compleasm_2023}), resulting in a completeness of 95.66\% 
(\texttt{S}:45.31\%; \texttt{D}:50.35\%; \texttt{F}:0.79\%; \texttt{M}:3.55\%; 
\texttt{N}:1,013) for the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} single-copy ortholog set 
(Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). The statistics for this initial assembly, with 
twice the expected genome size and over 50\% duplicated \texttt{BUSCO}s, 
suggested the presence of a large fraction of haplotig sequences, reflecting 
the high heterozygosity observed for the species.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following optimization, both in the integration of additional data types and 
in the increased stringency of the purging of haplotig and contaminant 
sequences (see Methods), we generated a contig-level assembly composed of 
1,342 sequences, with a total length of 1.05 Gbp, a contig N50 of 1.35 Mbp, 
and a \texttt{BUSCO} completeness of 93.88\% ([\texttt{S}:89.04\%; 
\texttt{D}:4.84\%; \texttt{F}:1.18\%; \texttt{M}:4.94\%; \texttt{N}:1,013], 
\texttt{arthropoda\_odb10}). The contig-level assembly was then scaffolded 
with Hi-C data using the \texttt{YaHS} software (\cite{zhou_yahs_2023}; 
Figure~\ref{fig:sup_hic}). Our scaffolding process generated an assembly 
with a total length of 1.05 Gbp, composed of 650 scaffolds, a scaffold N50 
of 50.96 Mbp, and a \texttt{BUSCO} completeness of 93.98\% ([\texttt{S}:89.24\%; 
\texttt{D}:4.74\%; \texttt{F}:0.89\%; \texttt{M}:5.13\%; \texttt{N}:1,013], 
\texttt{arthropoda\_odb10}) (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

This scaffold-level assembly was further processed by performing manual curation 
of the Hi-C contact map and self-correction using the software \texttt{Inspector} 
(\cite{chen_accurate_2021}). This curated assembly was composed of 592 fragments, 
a total sequence length of 1.04 Gbp, a scaffold N50 of 51.41 Mbp, and a per-base 
quality value (QV) of 30.49. When assessing completeness, the curated assembly was 
94.08\% (\texttt{S}:89.44\%; \texttt{D}:4.64\%; \texttt{F}:0.89\%; \texttt{M}:5.03\%; 
\texttt{N}:1,013) and 92.77\% (\texttt{S}:87.77\%; \texttt{D}:5.40\%; 
\texttt{F}:2.93\%; \texttt{M}:4.30\%; \texttt{N}:1,536) gene-complete for the 
\texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} \texttt{BUSCO} datasets, 
respectively (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). Of the 1.04 Gbp of total sequence, 
906.6 Mbp (86.74\%) was contained in 58 scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp. Moreover, 
the 16 largest sequences (larger than 8.5 Mbp) contain 841.2 Mbp (80.61\%) of 
the total sequence, and likely represent 16 putative chromosomes. This karyotype 
number, N=16, is in agreement with that observed for other species in the genus 
\textit{Balanus} (\cite{austin_chromosome_1958}) and other acorn barnacles in the 
family Balanidae (\cite{han_new_2024}).

\subsection*{Genome annotation}

% Stats and QC on the annotation.

Once the assembly and scaffolding was completed, we annotated and masked repeat 
sequences in the \textit{B. glandula} reference assembly using the \texttt{EarlGrey} 
software (\cite{baril_earl_2024}). This analysis identified 698.6 Mbp (66.94\%) 
of the assembly as repetitive elements (Table~\ref{tab:sup_earlgrey_summary}); 
however, the majority of these, 404.5 Mbp (38.76\% of the assembly), remain 
unclassified among the major repeat element types. This large proportion of 
unclassified repeat elements is likely the product of the small number of 
representative barnacle sequences among curated repeat reference libraries, 
reflecting barnacle's status as a genomically understudied group. Among the 
classified repeats, DNA and LINE elements were the two most abundant repeats 
classes, comprising 8.45\% and 9.16\% of the total assembly, respectively.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following repeat masking of the reference assembly, we annotated protein-coding 
genes using short-read RNAseq and long-read PacBio Iso-Seq sequencing, combining 
and curating annotations generated across the two data types (see Methods). This 
process resulted in 20,234 protein-coding genes annotated in the genome. The 
resulting annotation was 93.58\% (\texttt{S}:79.76\%; \texttt{D}:13.82\%; 
\texttt{F}:1.78\%; \texttt{M}:4.64\%; \texttt{N}:1,013) and 84.77\% 
(\texttt{S}:72.66\%; \texttt{D}:12.11\%; \texttt{F}:4.10\%; \texttt{M}:11.13\%; 
\texttt{N}:1,536) gene-complete when compared against the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} 
and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} \texttt{BUSCO} datasets, respectively. Additionally, 
we compared our annotation against the proteome of four other barnacle species 
(see Methods) using \texttt{OrthoFinder} (\cite{emms_orthofinder_2015,
emms_orthofinder_2019}), resulting in the assignment of 16,978 (83.91\%) 
\textit{B. glandula} genes to orthologous groups across barnacles and the 
identification of 2,576 single-copy orthologs across all five species.

In addition to protein-coding genes, we also annotated non-coding elements in the 
\textit{B. glandula} reference assembly. We identified 7,532 long, non-coding RNAs 
(lncRNA), 1,203 transfer RNAs (tRNA), and 24 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. In total,

\subsection*{Conserved architecture across barnacle genomes}

We performed a conserved synteny analysis using the software \texttt{Genespace} 
(\cite{lovell_genespace_2022}) to identify patterns of large-scale structure across 
barnacle genomes. We compared our \textit{B. glandula} genome against the 
chromosome-level genome assemblies of the striped barnacle \textit{Amphibalanus 
amphitrite} (Balanidae; \cite{han_new_2024}) and the gooseneck barnacle 
\textit{Pollicipes pollicipes} (Pollicipedidae; \cite{bernot_chromosome-level_2022}). 
These genomes display conservation in their large-scale organization, including 
one-to-one correspondence between 16 putatively orthologous chromosomes across the 
three species (Figure~\ref{fig:synteny}). This finding shows that our scaffolding 
of the \textit{B. glandula} assembly likely reflects the biological structure of 
the genome and is not the product of technical artifacts, but also suggests the 
conservation in the genome architecture of barnacle genomes across $\sim$165 Mya 
of evolution (\cite{han_new_2024,bernot_chromosome-level_2022}).

\subsection*{High genetic diversity in the \textit{B. glandula} reference assembly}

We calculated estimates of diversity present in the barnacle genome by genotyping 
our \textit{B. glandula} reference individual. After filtering low-quality genotypes 
and masking regions with annotated repeats, we obtained genotypes for 314 million 
sites along the genome, equivalent to 34.71\% of the 904.94 Mbp present in the 58 
scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp (Table~\ref{tab:sup_popgen_stats}). Among these 314 
million genotyped sites, 9.08 million (2.89\%) were heterozygous in the reference 
individual, including 8.73 million SNPs and 356 thousand indels. In total, this 
variation represent a mean density of 14.66 heterozygous sites per kbp (median: 6; 
standard deviation: 19.86). Moreover, we observed a total of 399 thousand variant 
sites within the span of protein-coding sequences, including 144 thousand 
non-synonymous point mutations, as categorized by the \texttt{snpEff} software 
(\cite{cingolani_program_2012}). In this reference individual, the average 
transcript showed a mean number of 7.74 (median: 3, standard deviation: 14.97) 
non-synonymous variants, indicating a substantial proportion of coding variation 
in just a single barnacle individual.

\subsection*{Functional elements are conserved in barnacle genomes}

% For phastCons, mostly saying how we found the hughly conserved stuff and what 
% it is. The enrichment results are generally expected (tRNAs and CDSs are conserved). 
% Not sure what else to say there.

We used the \texttt{phastCons} software (\cite{siepel_evolutionarily_2005}) on the 
\texttt{cactus} whole-genome alignment (\cite{armstrong_progressive_2020}) between 
six barnacle species to identify highly conserved sites in the Pacific acorn barnacle 
genome. Across the 16 putative chromosomes in the \textit{B. glandula} assembly, 
\texttt{phastCons} identified 196,141 highly-conserved elements (HCEs), spanning 
50.85 Mbp of sequence and 6.05\% of the sequence spanning the 16 assembled chromosomes.
In \textit{B. glandula}, the HCEs had a mean length of 259.25 bp (median: 42 bp, 
standard deviation: 990.40 bp).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following the identification of HCEs, we compared their sequence composition against 
the genome-wide background (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}). For this, we first overlaid 
the genomic coordinates of the HCEs against the \textit{B. glandula} annotation, 
obtaining the proportion of annotated features among HCE sites (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}A). 
Among our target features, including an ``intergenic'' and ``multiple'' class for 
the unannotated fraction of the genome and annotation overlaps (e.g., a site spanning 
both a transposable element and an intron), respectively, all but one is present 
among HCEs. We do not observe any rRNA sites among our conserved sites; however, this 
is likely the product of the whole-genome alignment, given rRNA's repetitive nature 
and alignment difficulty (\cite{gillespie_characterizing_2004}), and not an accurate 
measure of conservation (or lack therof). Aside from the absence of rRNAs, the 
sequence composition of HCE largely follows that of the whole genome. Most annotated 
features do not appear substantially enriched among HCE sites (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}B, 
Table~\ref{tab:sup_phastcons_enrichment}). The two clear exceptions are coding 
sequences (CDSs) and tRNAs. CDSs display an average two-fold enrichment among HCEs 
(mean $\log_2$ enrichment: 0.75, median: 0.98, standard deviation: 0.93), reflecting 
the conservation of protein-coding genes among barnacle genomes. Similarly, tRNAs exhibit 
four-fold enrichment in HCEs (mean $\log_2$ enrichment: 2.14, median: 2.04, standard 
deviation: 0.96), the likely product of the strong functional constraint expected for 
these biologically-important sequences.

\subsection*{Patterns of molecular evolution reflect strong positive selection in 
protein-coding genes}

To look at the rate of molecular evolution in protein coding sequences, we calculated 
at the pairwise rate of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions 
(d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S}) between \textit{B. glandula} and its 
congener, the wrinkled barnacle \textit{Balanus crenatus}. We identified and aligned 
9,082 single-copy orthologs between both species. The mean pairwise 
d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} was 0.206 (median: 0.158, standard deviation: 
0.191; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dnds}). This ratio indicates that, on average and under 
this specific metric, coding sequencing in these barnacle genomes are under purifying 
selection when compared to outgroup sequences, reflecting the expected evolutionary 
constraints over sequences of functional importance.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Of the 9 thousand genes assessed, 70 (0.77\%) displayed a 
d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} $>$ 1, and are likely candidates under positive 
selection. The predicted function of the majority ($>80\%$) of these genes is 
unknown, as determined by the functional annotation generated by the \texttt{EnTap} 
software (\cite{hart_span_2020}). This is expected, since homology is likely harder 
to determine for fast-evolving sequences putatively under strong selection. 
Regardless, we found several genes with known function in our candidates under 
positive selection. Among then, we found a follistatin-A-like homolog, known to be 
involved to myogenesis and muscle development in crustaceans 
(\cite{yan_identification_2021}). We also observe a homolog of the 
gamma-interferon-inducible lysosomal thiol reductase, known to be involved in innate 
immune response in crustaceans (\cite{liu_involvement_2019}). Additionally, we find 
homologs to tumor protein p53-inducible protein 11 (which is involved in cell cycle 
regulation), chymotrypsin-like protease CTRL-1, and the protein quiver gene.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We expanded on our d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} measures of positive 
selection in protein-coding sequences in the \textit{B. glandula} genome by 
incorporating population-level polymorphism using the McDonald-Kreitman test 
(\cite{mcdonald_adaptive_1991}). To achieve this, we incorporated genetic variants 
observed in a \textit{B. glandula} population by taking genetic variants present 
across the whole-genome of seven individuals (including our reference individual and 
six additional samples, see below), and comparing those against the orthologous 
coding sequences in the \textit{B. crenatus} outgroup.
\vspace{0.2cm}

After filtering alignments both by length ($<100$ bp) and by the presence of 
premature stop codons and frameshift mutations, we calculated the McDonald-Kreitman 
tests for 7,714 orthologs sequences between \textit{B. glandula} and \textit{B. 
crenatus}. Four-hundred and seventeen (417, 5.41\%) of these orthologs showed a 
significant deviation from the expected proportion of non-synonymous and synonymous 
substitutions and polymorphisms, following \textit{p}-value correction 
(Figure~\ref{fig:mkTest}). We additionally calculated the Neutrality Index (NI; 
\cite{rand_excess_1996}) and Direction of Selection (DoS; \cite{stoletzki_estimation_2011}) 
at each ortholog. We observe a mean NI value of 0.745 (median: 0.554; standard 
deviation: 1.079) and a mean DoS of 0.102 (median: 0.106; standard deviation: 0.131; 
Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dos}). Both of these statistics show that, on average and in contrast 
with our measurements of d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S}, protein coding 
sequences in \textit{B. glandula} exhibit an excess of non-synonymous substitutions, 
suggesting that they are evolving under positive directional selection when compared 
to the outgroup sequence.
\vspace{0.2cm}

In addition to NI and DoS, we also calculated \textalpha{}, a measure of the proportion 
of non-synonymous substitutions fixed by positive selection (\cite{smith_adaptive_2002}). 
We calculated two adjusted versions of \textalpha{} to account for the presence of known biases. 
First, we calculated the Tarone-Greenland \textalpha{} estimator (\textalpha{}\textsubscript{TG}), 
a weighted estimator that correct for the effects of averaging over a large number of genes 
(\cite{stoletzki_estimation_2011}). Across our 7,714 genes, we calculated an 
\textalpha{}\textsubscript{TG} of 0.530 ($95\%$ Confidence Interval: 0.523--0.538), which 
indicates that 53\% of non-synonymous substitutions in this \textit{B. glandula} population 
were fixed as a product of positive selection. Secondly, we calculated the \textalpha{} 
product of the asymptotic McDonald-Kreitman test (\textalpha{}\textsubscript{asym}), which 
corrects for the presence of weakly deleterious non-synonymous polymorphism present at 
low frequency in the population (\cite{messer_frequent_2013}). We found a mean 
\textalpha{}\textsubscript{asym} of 0.610 ($95\%$ Confidence Interval: 0.600--0.620). 
Regardless of the metric used, both \textalpha{}\textsubscript{TG} and 
\textalpha{}\textsubscript{asym} agree that the majority of non-synonymous substitutions 
in this barnacle population are the outcome of directional positive selection.

\subsection*{Barnacle genes show a biased use of non-synonymous codons}

We used the annotated transcript sequences of our Pacific acorn barnacle genome 
to calculate the effective number of codons (ENC), a measure of the extent of 
departure of equal usage across synonymous codons (\cite{wright_effective_1990,
sun_improved_2013}). We successfully calculated ENC across the coding sequences of 
16,904 \textit{B. glandula} genes, finding an average (mean) number of 43.1 
effective codons (median: 42.7, standard deviation: 5.56, Figure~\ref{fig:codon_enc}). 
This ENC distribution highlights a substantial reduction in the usage of synonymous 
codons, higher than that observed for \textit{Drosophila} (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dmel_enc}),
suggesting that some synonymous substitutions may not be neutral in this large
barnacle population.

\subsection*{Barnacle populations exhibit elevated genetic polymorphism}

We sequenced the whole genomes of six \textit{B. glandula} individuals collected 
from Cape Perpetua, in the central Oregon coast. After a stringent filtering of sites 
with low coverage, low quality, and/or missing genotypes, we retained a total of 
111.6 million genotyped sites (Table~\ref{tab:sup_popgen_stats}), 21.4 million 
(19.16\%) of which were variant in the sequenced individuals. Among these variant 
sites, we recovered 20.5 million point mutations, including 996 thousand SNPs that 
were multiallelic in this set of just six individuals. In total, the amount of 
polymorphism observed in our sampling translates to a mean density of 41.05 variant 
sites per kbp (median: 29, standard deviation: 40.82). Moreover, 1.12 million of 
these variant sites (5.23\%) were located within the span of coding sequences. These 
coding variants included 360 thousand missense (non-synonymous) mutations, 
corresponding to an average (mean) of 18.93 non-synonymous variants per transcript 
(median: 10, standard deviation: 28.50). Overall, these represent a substantial 
proportion of coding variation segregating in this central Oregon population. Moreover, 
this \textit{B. glandula} population displayed a mean genome-wide nucleotide 
diversity (\textpi{}) of 0.0506 (median: 0.0531, standard deviation: 0.0188; 
Figure~\ref{fig:genome_pi}; Table~\ref{tab:sup_feature_pi}). This degree of 
diversity is an order of magnitude higher than that observed in diverse 
\textit{Drosophila} populations in Sub-Saharan Africa (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dmel_pi}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Along each chromosome, we did not observe any strong correlation between the density 
of specific genomic features (e.g., CDSs, lncRNAs, tRNAs, introns, and repeats) and 
\textpi{}, particularly not at the scale of 10 kbp (Figure~\ref{fig:genome_pi}; 
Figure~\ref{fig:sup_pi_pairplot}). However, stronger relationships are likely present 
at smaller genomic intervals (see below). The strongest correlation was, expectedly, 
observed between the density of segregating sites along 10 kbp windows and \textpi{} 
(Spearman's $\rho$: 0.706; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_pi_pairplot}). The proportion of 
segregating sites in itself was not correlated with the density of particular genomic 
features, except for repeats; however, this is likely an artifact of the masking of 
genotypes located within repetitive intervals in the reference (see Methods).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Looking at diversity across the genome, we observe an overall reduction in \textpi{} 
at putatively functional sites. For example, the mean \textpi{} in sequences spanning 
annotated CDSs is 0.0302 (median: 0.0270, standard deviation: 0.0221; 
Figure~\ref{fig:feature_pi}; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_pi_hist}; Table~\ref{tab:sup_feature_pi}),
which represents a $\sim$ 1.6x reduction in diversity when compared to the 
genome-wide average. A reduction in diversity of similar magnitude is also observed 
in the sequences of lncRNAs (1.4x) and 5' UTRs (1.3x). The largest reduction in 
diversity when compared to the genome-wide background was observed in tRNAs, with 
\textpi{} $\sim$ 4.3x lower than the genomic background and in agreement with their 
high functional constrained as observed from their enrichment in highly-conserved 
elements in the genome. In contrast to most other elements of the genome, introns 
display a relative increase in diversity, with a mean \textpi{} of 0.0646 (median: 
0.0650, standard deviation: 0.0299), $\sim$ 1.3x higher than the genome-wide average.

Zooming in further on coding sequences, we further explored the patterns of \textpi{} 
at 0-fold and 4-fold degenerate sites, effectively comparing diversity at constrained 
versus neutral (or putatively neutral) sites. In this central Oregon population of 
\textit{B. glandula}, the mean \textpi{} at 0-fold degenerate sites \textendash{} in

\subsection*{\textit{B. glandula} exhibits large effective population sizes}

We estimated the effective population size (N\textsubscript{e}) of this central 
Oregon \textit{B. glandula} population using the whole-genome genotypes. First, we 
calculated N\textsubscript{e} using the Watterson's estimator of genetic diversity 
($\theta_W$), observing a genome-wide mean $\theta_W$ of 0.0617 (median: 0.0635, 
standard deviation: 0.0224; Figure~\ref{fig:sup_theta_ne}). Using the relationship 
between the effective population size and the Watterson's estimator, 
$\theta_W=4N_{e}\mu$, we calculated the mean effective size of this population as 
5,137,172 (median: 5,289,211, standard deviation: 1,862,683), according to a mutation 
rate per-base, per-generation ($\mu$) of $3\times10^{-9}$ (as estimated by 
\cite{nunez_footprints_2020} for the acorn barnacle \textit{S. balanoides}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Additionally, we did a coalescent-based estimation of N\textsubscript{e} through 
time using \texttt{MSMC2} (\cite{dutheil_msmc_2020}). This estimate also placed the 
historical (between $10^{6}$ and $10^{7}$ generations ago) effective size of the 
population in the magnitude of $10^{6}$ effective individuals 
(Figure~\ref{fig:sup_mcmc_ne}); however, with our small sampling and estimating using 
only pairs of haplotypes per-individual, we were unable to obtain accurate 
N\textsubscript{e} estimates at more recent ($<10^{5}$) estimates.

\subsection*{Genome assembly in highly polymorphic species}

% The idea here is to say that we have all these improvement in assembly and data 
% analysis for genomes. These are all good, but not really applicable for these very 
% diverse system. Just highlighting the gap in knowledge and the need for improvements 
% in the field. Good hook for cyclothone and other projects.

Improvements in the affordability, accessibility, and accuracy in sequencing 
technologies has increased the generation of high quality reference genomes across 
the tree of life. Over the last decade, we have seen the research outcomes of large 
genome assembly consortiums, e.g., the Vertebrate Genome Project 
(\cite{rhie_towards_2021}) and the Earth BioGenome Project 
(\cite{blaxter_earth_2025}), tackling the production of high quality genomes across 
diverse taxa. We have also seen the generation of complete genomes, sequencing 
chromosomes at a telomore-to-telomere scale (\cite{nurk_complete_2022,
yoo_complete_2025}), as well as the sequencing and assembly of some of the largest 
and most repetitive genomes known in nature (\cite{campos-dominguez_genome_2025}). 
Alongside these advances, there has also been access to cutting-edge best practice 
protocols for the application of these analyses to new taxa in a reproducible fashion 
(\cite{lawniczak_best-practice_2025,li_genome_2024}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Despite these advances, there appears to be a gap in knowledge in the analysis of 
highly polymorphic organisms, such as barnacles. High heterozygosity presents 
problems during the assembly process, e.g., hindering the differentiation between 
paralogs and haplotypes, which often results in the inflation of the genome size and 
a high proportion of duplicates sequences (\cite{guiglielmoni_phasing_2024,
asalone_regional_2020,roach_purge_2018,mochizuki_practical_2023}). The most common 
approach is to manage these haplotypes \textit{a posteriori}, purging them after 
initial assembly (\cite{guan_identifying_2020,roach_purge_2018}); however, while this 
approach can be successful in generating haploid assemblies, as we have shown here, 
it is not without its drawbacks. First, purging haplotypes could lead to a reduction 
in the gene completeness in the main assembly, as primary haplotypes might be 
incorrectly labelled as haplotigs, leading to their removal. Secondly, the ability 
to identify and remove haplotypes is dependent on the properties of the assembly. 
Sequences that are too fragmented and/or two divergent from one another are likely 
harder to purge due to difficulties in mapping. Moreover, when performing phased 
diploid assemblies, the excessive presence of haplotypes will interfere with 
phasing, leading to a main haploid assembly that is a mosaic between the two 
haplotypes and/or the poor quality of the secondary assembly.
\vspace{0.2cm}

These difficulties drive the need for the development of methods and analysis best 
practices focused on the assembly of highly heterozygous genomes, and for a further 
exploration of how elevated heterozygosity affects \textit{de novo} assembly 
parameters. While some improvements have been made in recent years, e.g., in 
\texttt{hifiasm} with the direct purging of haplotigs during assembly and the 
integration of Hi-C data to phase haplotypes without the need of parental genomes 
(\cite{cheng_haplotype-resolved_2022}), further work is likely needed before we can 
obtain haplotype-resolved genome assemblies for hyper polymorphic organisms.

\subsection*{The magnitude of genetic diversity in barnacle populations}

Our work here shows that a Pacific acorn barnacle population from the central Oregon 
coast exhibits genetic diversity of over 5\% (mean genome-wide \textpi{} of 0.0506). 
These levels of genetic diversity are higher than those observed for other model 
systems known for their relatively large population sizes. For example, the 
genome-wide nucleotide diversity we observe here in barnacles is an order of magnitude 
higher than that observed for \textit{Drosophila} (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_dmel_pi}), both 
at a local scale and diversity observed in large-scale surveys (\cite{nunez_footprints_2025,
li_comparative_2025,pool_population_2012}). Even the diversity observed at 
zero-fold degenerate sites, our least diverse in the genome as the likely product of 
functional constraint, is still an order of magnitude higher than the diverse 
observed in these diverse \textit{Drosophila} populations.

\subsection*{Barnacles as models for evolution at biological extremes}

% Now, bringing the hyperpolymorphic stuff. We are above the "cutfoff" of 
% hyperpolymorphic. But also, the Hart2025 "limit" of 1% for species boundaries. 
% We are past that here.

The magnitude of diversity observed in barnacles solidly places them among 
hyper-polymorphic populations, those who exhibit diversity above 5\% 
(\cite{cutter_molecular_2013}). This adds barnacles to growing list of emerging 
hyperpolymorphic models systems, which are key to studying the limits of biological 
variation and molecular in natural populations. Moreover, our finding of diversity 
also has important implications for the study of species boundaries. For example, 
recent work by \cite{hart_genomic_2025} denotes how levels of above 1\% neutral 
diversity in Eukaryotes likely serve as biological limits for the definition of 
species. Barnacles and other hyperpolymorphic populations puts these putative 
cutoffs into question. We observe levels of genome-wide diversity of 5\% in just 
a very small portion of the biological range of the Pacific acorn barnacle. 
Given the species' broader geographical and ecological distribution, genetic 
diversity is likely to increase when looking across the whole range. This 
observation reiterates the importance of future studies of genetic diversity 
of this species over a broader geographic range, providing some insights on 
the mechanisms that maintain population and species boundaries in the presence 
of extreme genetic diversity.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Moreover, barnacles appear to be an emerging model for the study of the dynamics 
between effective and census population sizes. From our results on diversity, we 
observe that this barnacle population has an effective size approximately $10^{6}$. 
However, these populations also exhibit densities of $10^{4}$ individuals per $m^{2}$ 
(\cite{menge_recruitment_2000}). Based on this estimate, the census size of the Pacific 
barnacle population in central Oregon should be in the billions (if not more) of 
barnacles. While the general disparity between these two population size estimates has 
been long known, i.e., Lewontin's Paradox (\cite{lewontin_genetic_1974}), the 
mechanisms underlying the differences have been long debated (\cite{gillespie_role_1999,
gillespie_genetic_2000, smith_hitch-hiking_1974,kimura_neutral_1983}) and are still 
the subjects of study (\cite{buffalo_quantifying_2021}). Our work on the Pacific 
acorn barnacle provides an opportunity to study these process empirically.

\section*{Conclusion}

In this work, we describe the first high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly 
for the Pacific acorn barnacle, \textit{Balanus glandula}. Using this new assembly, 
we first identify conserved synteny among barnacle genomes despite 165 Mya of 
divergence. Functional elements are highly conserved in the genome of the 
species; however, patterns of molecular evolution highlight the role of 
positive selection as a key driver in the divergence of coding sequences between 
closely related barnacle species. At a population level, Oregon populations of 
\textit{B. glandula} appear to be hyperpolymorphic, showing levels of genetic 
diversity of over 5\% along the genome. Yet, we find preliminary evidence of 
background selection over protein-coding and non-coding genes, reiterating the 
functional constraint genes. Overall, our work highlights the role of the 
Pacific acorn barnacle as an emerging model for the study of genetic variation 
and the mechanisms of evolution at biological extremes.

glandula} individuals from the docks in the Oregon Institute of Marine Biology,

PacBio Sequel II machine at the University of Oregon's Genomics and Cell 
Characterization Core Facility (GC3F). These three sequencing runs yielded a total 
of 5.93 million reads with a read N50 of 12.4 kbp.

\subsection*{Genome assembly}

\subsubsection*{Genome size and heterozygosity estimation}

We estimated the genome size and heterozygosity of the \textit{B. glandula} 
genome using k-mers. First, we used the \texttt{count} command of the 
\texttt{jellyfish} software version \texttt{2.2.10} (\cite{marcais_fast_2011}) 
to count the unique k-mers present in the PacBio HiFi reads. We counted k-mers 
of size 21 (\texttt{--mer-len 21}) present in both strands (\texttt{--canonical}). 
From these counts, we then generated an empirical k-mer distribution using the 
\texttt{jellyfish histo} command. Finally, we used the software 
\texttt{GenomeScope2} version \texttt{2.0} (\cite{ranallo-benavidez_genomescope_2020}) 
to model the distribution of k-mers in the diploid genome (\texttt{--ploidy 2}) 
based on the empirical counts.

\subsubsection*{Base contig-level genome assembly}

A base, contig-level genome assembly for \textit{B. glandula} was generated from 
the PacBio HiFi reads using \texttt{hifiasm} version \texttt{0.19.6-r595} 
(\cite{cheng_haplotype-resolved_2021,cheng_haplotype-resolved_2022}) with default 
parameters. This base assembly was assesed for contiguity using \texttt{quast} 
version \texttt{5.2.0} (\cite{gurevich_quast_2013}), and for gene-completeness with 
\texttt{compleasm} version \texttt{0.12-r237} (\cite{huang_compleasm_2023}) using 
the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} reference \texttt{BUSCO} dataset (\cite{simao_busco_2015,
manni_busco_2021,tegenfeldt_orthodb_2025}).

\subsubsection*{Assembly decontamination}

Following the generation of a base, contig-level genome assembly, we detected 
endogenous contamination (i.e., sequences not belonging to \textit{B. glandula}) 
in both reads and assembled contigs using the \texttt{blobtoolkit} software version 
\texttt{4.3.0} (\cite{challis_blobtoolkit_2020}). To achieve this, we first created 
a new \texttt{blobtools} database, using the \texttt{create} command, specifying the 
contig-level assembly as our reference sequence (\texttt{--fasta}), the NCBI 
taxonomical identifier for \textit{B. glandula} (\texttt{--taxid 110520}), and 
the NCBI reference taxonomy files (\texttt{--taxdump}, downloaded on 2023/12/13).
\vspace{0.2cm}

To add depth of coverage to this database, we aligned our PacBio HiFi sequencing 
reads to our base, contig-level reference assembly using \texttt{minimap2} version 
\texttt{2.26-r1175} (\cite{li_minimap2_2018}), running in the \texttt{map-hifi} 
configuration (\texttt{-x map-hifi}) and exporting alignments in SAM format 
(\texttt{-a}). Alignments were then processed, sorted, and indexed using 
\texttt{samtools} version \texttt{1.18} (\cite{li_sequence_2009}). The processed 
alignments were then added to the \texttt{blobtools} database using the \texttt{add} 
command.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We did a taxonomical assignment of each sequence in the assembly by first aligning 
our contigs against NCBI's non-redundant database (downloaded on 2023/12/13). We 
aligned each contig against the database using \texttt{blastn} version 
\texttt{2.15.0+}, as implemented in \texttt{BLAST+} (\cite{camacho_blast_2009}), 
specifying a minimum e-value cutoff (\texttt{-evalue}) of $1\times10^{-10}$, a 
maximum of 25 target sequence exported in the output (\texttt{-max\_target\_seqs 25}), 
and exporting in tabular format (\texttt{-outfmt 6}). These BLAST allginments 
were then added to the \texttt{blobtools} database using the \texttt{add} command, 
specifying the rule for taxonomical assignment (\texttt{--taxrule bestsumorder}). 
Using this approach, the \texttt{blobtools} database provides a single taxonomic 
assignment to each assembled contig.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following taxonomical assignment of the assembled contigs, we used the 
\texttt{blobtools filter} command to obtain the IDs of sequencing reads that were 
assigned to a given taxon. We specified paths of our reference contigs 
(\texttt{--fasta}), alignments (\texttt{--cov}), and sequencing reads 
(\texttt{--fastq}). For filtering, we specified the parameters to select the 
reads aligned to contigs assigned to the class Thecostraca (\texttt{--Keys=
Thecostraca}) using a given taxonomic assignment rule (\texttt{--param 
bestsumorder\_class}). We then specified the (\texttt{--invert}) option to specify 
that we wished to export the IDs of reads matching this assignment (i.e., keeping 
only reads specific to the barnacle class Thecostraca).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Lastly, we used the IDs of the Thecostraca-assigned reads to filter our raw 
PacBio HiFi FASTQ file using the \texttt{subseq} command from the \texttt{seqtk} 
software version \texttt{1.4-r130} (\url{https://github.com/lh3/seqtk}). This 
filtering in 5.74 million Thecostraca-specific PacBio HiFi reads, representing 
96.8\% of the total sequencing library. This taxonomic assignment and read 
filtering was also performed on the ONT reads, retaining 371 thousand (95.8\%) 
reads.

\subsubsection*{Optimized contig-level genome assembly}

We generated a new contig-level genome assembly using the Thecostraca-specific 
data and iterating over different assembly parameters and filtering schemes, 
selecting an optimal assembly based on the best contiguity and gene-completeness 
metrics (\cite{rayamajhi_evaluating_2022}), as determined by \texttt{quast} and 
\texttt{compleasm}, respectively. This optimized assembly was generated using 
\texttt{hifiasm} version \texttt{0.19.8-r603}, including our Thecostraca-specific 
PacBio HiFi reads as well as the ultra-long ONT (\texttt{--ul}) and Hi-C reads 
(\texttt{--h1}, \texttt{--h2}). This assembly was done using using an estimated 
haploid genome size of 800 Mbp (\texttt{--hg-size 800m}), dropping k-mers observed 
over ten times the average coverage (\texttt{-D 10.0}), and allowing scaffolding 
(\texttt{--dual-scaf}). For identifying and correctly phasing haplotype sequences, 
we set the expected coverage at homozygous sites (\texttt{--hom-cov 68}) and the 
upper bound of coverage to purging duplicates (\texttt{--purge-max 68}) at 68x, 
along with setting the similarity threshold between duplicate haplotigs to 25\% 
(\texttt{-s 0.25}). This assembly was composed of 2,218 contigs, with a total 
length of 1.29 Gbp, and a contig N50 of 1.16 Mbp.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following the initial assembly of contigs, we purged haplotig sequences using the 
\texttt{purge\_dups} software version \texttt{1.2.5} (\cite{guan_identifying_2020}). 
Following the \texttt{purge\_dups} documentation, we first mapped the PacBio HiFi 
reads to this contig-level assembly using \texttt{minimap2 -x map-hifi}. We then 
calculated the read-depth histogram using the \texttt{pbcstat} command, following 
by determining the base-level coverage cutoffs using the command \texttt{calcuts}. 
Subsequently, we split the assembly at gaps, if present, using the \texttt{split\_fa} 
command, followed by a self-alignment using \texttt{minimap2 -x asm5}. The 
\texttt{purge\_dups} command was then used to mark haplotig sequences in a BED 
file according to the coverage cutoffs empirically determined from the data. 
Lastly, the assembly FASTA was processed to remove sequences tagged as haplotigs 
using the \texttt{get\_seqs} command, only removing sequences at the ends of contigs 
(\texttt{-e}) and allowing the splitting of contigs (\texttt{-s}). The purging 
process removed 876 fragments from the assembly, accounting for 240 Mbp of haplotig 
sequences (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).

\subsubsection*{Hi-C Scaffolding}

We scaffolded the purged contig-level assembly into chromosome-level scaffolds using 
Hi-C data. First, we aligned the Hi-C short-read data to our assembly using 
\texttt{bwa mem} version \texttt{0.7.17-r1188} (\cite{li_aligning_2013}), setting the 
minimum alignment score to output to zero (\texttt{-T 0}), skipping make rescue 
(\texttt{-S}), mate pairing (\texttt{-P}), and setting the smallest query coordinate 
as primary for split alignments (\texttt{-5}). Alignments were converted to BAM format 
using \texttt{samtools} version \texttt{1.18}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

We processed the aligned Hi-C data using the \texttt{pairtools} version 
\texttt{1.0.2} software (\cite{open2c_pairtools_2023}), following the Dovetail analysis 
pipeline (\url{https://dovetail-analysis.readthedocs.io}). Briefly, we recorded valid 
ligation events using \texttt{pairtools parse}, setting a minimum mapping quality of 40 
(\texttt{--min-mapq 40}), a maximum inter-alignment gap of 30 kbp 
(\texttt{--max-inter-align-gap 30}), and reporting the 5'-most unique alignment when 
detecting multiple ligation events (\texttt{--walks-policy 5unique}). We then sorted 
the processed alignment pairs using \texttt{pairtools sort}, and marked PCR duplicate 
reads using \texttt{pairtools dedup --mark-dups}. Finally, we used \texttt{pairtools 
split} to process the data into a \texttt{BAM} file of valid alignments and a 
\texttt{pairs} file with the read pairs forming the contact map. The resulting alignment 
\texttt{BAM} was sorted and indexed using \texttt{samtools}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

The processed Hi-C alignments were used to scaffold the genome using the \texttt{YaHS} 
software version \texttt{1.2a.1} (\cite{zhou_yahs_2023}). Since our assembly was in 
highly fragmented contigs, we reduced the minimum resolution size to 1 kbp (\texttt{-r 
1000}). Following the \texttt{YaHS} documentation, we generated a Hi-C contact map 
using the \texttt{juicer} suite of tools (\cite{durand_juicer_2016,dudchenko_juicebox_2018}). 
First, we used \texttt{juicer} version \texttt{1.1} \texttt{pre} command to convert 
the \texttt{YaHS} output into the \texttt{juicer} format in assembly mode 
(\texttt{-a}). We then ran \texttt{juicer tools} version \texttt{1.9.9} to generate 
the \texttt{*.assembly} and \texttt{*.hic} files required for manual curation using 
the \texttt{Juicebox} GUI version \texttt{2.17.00}. Following manual curation of 
the contact map, we regenerated the assembly using \texttt{juicer post}. This scaffolded 
assembly was composed of 650 fragments, a total length of 1.05 Gbp, and a scaffold N50 
of 50.96 Mbp (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).

\subsubsection*{Polishing and correcting}

After manual curation of the scaffolded genome, we performed one round of polishing and 
automatic curation using the \texttt{Inspector} software version \texttt{1.0.1} 
(\cite{chen_accurate_2021}). First, we evaluated the assembly using \texttt{inspector.py}, 
mapping the PacBio HiFi reads against the assembly. The assembly was then corrected using 
\texttt{inspector-correct.py}.

\subsection*{Genome annotation}

\subsubsection*{Repeat annotation}

We identified and classified repetitive elements in the \textit{B. glandula} genome 
using the \texttt{EarlGrey} software version \texttt{4.1.0} (\cite{baril_earl_2024}). 
Before running \texttt{EarlGrey}, we first downloaded the \texttt{Dfam 3.8} database 
(\cite{storer_dfam_2021}), extracting the annotated arthropod repeat sequences to generate 
an initial consensus masking library. We provided this initial consensus library to 
\texttt{EarlGrey} (\texttt{-l}). We also used ``arthropoda'' as the search term for 
\texttt{RepeatMasker} version \texttt{4.1.2} (\cite{smit_repeatmasker_2013}). In addition, 
we clustered the final TE library to reduce redundancy (\texttt{-c yes}) and generated a 
soft-masked genome (\texttt{-d yes}).

\subsubsection*{Annotation of protein-coding genes}

After masking the repetitive sequences in the genome, we took an integrative approach to 
annotate protein-coding genes, combining evidence from both short-read RNAseq data, PacBio 
IsoSeq long reads, and protein sequences. For protein-level evidence, we downloaded the 
Arthropoda and Crustacea partitions of \texttt{OrthoDB} version \texttt{11} 
(\cite{kriventseva_orthodb_2019,zdobnov_orthodb_2021}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

When processing the short-read data, we first filtered and processed the raw RNAseq 
Illumina reads using \texttt{fastp} version \texttt{0.23.4} (\cite{chen_fastp_2018,
chen_ultrafast_2023}), setting the minimum length of reads post-filtering to 25 bp 
(\texttt{--length\_required 25}) and enabling the detection of adapter sequences in the 
paired reads (\texttt{--detect\_adapter\_for\_pe}). We then aligned the processed RNAseq 
reads to the \textit{B. glandula} assembly using \texttt{HISAT2} version \texttt{2.2.1} 
(\cite{kim_graph-based_2019}), exporting alignments tailored for \textit{de novo} 
transcriptome assemblers (\texttt{--dta}). The alignments were further processed and 
sorted using \texttt{samtools}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Using this aligned RNAseq data, we annotated protein-coding genes using \texttt{BRAKER} 
version \texttt{3.0.8} (\cite{gabriel_braker3_2024}). We ran \texttt{BRAKER} in \texttt{ETP} 
mode (\cite{bruna_genemark-etp_2024}), providing both RNAseq alignments (\texttt{--bams}) 
and Arthropod protein sequences (\texttt{--prot\_seq}) as evidence. We also used the 
Arthropod dataset from \texttt{BUSCO} (\cite{simao_busco_2015}) to enforce this set of 
orthologs as hints for \texttt{Augustus} (\cite{stanke_using_2008}) and reduce the number 
of missing single-copy orthologs in the final annotation (\texttt{--busco\_lineage 
arthropoda\_odb10}). After running \texttt{BUSCO}, we assessed the quality of the 
generated annotation using \texttt{compleasm} version \texttt{0.2.5} 
(\cite{huang_compleasm_2023}) in \text{protein} mode, using the Arthropod ortholog set 
as reference (\texttt{--lineage arthropoda\_odb10}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

In parallel, we annotated protein-coding genes using the PacBio IsoSeq data, aligning it 
to the assembly using \texttt{minimap2} version \texttt{2.26-r1175} (\texttt{-x splice:hq}) 
and processing the downstream alignments with \texttt{samtools}. We then annotated the 
genome using the long-read branch of \texttt{BRAKER} version \texttt{3.0.8}. Similar to 
the short-read annotation, we ran \texttt{BRAKER} in \texttt{ETP} mode, using the aligned 
IsoSeq data (\texttt{--bam}) and Arthropod protein sequences (\texttt{--prot\_seq}) as 
evidence, and enforcing the \texttt{BUSCO} Arthropod reference set in the annotation 
(\texttt{--busco\_lineage arthropoda\_odb10}). Lastly, we used \texttt{compleasm protein} 
to assess the completeness of the annotation.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Once the RNAseq- and IsoSeq-based \texttt{BRAKER} annotations were completed, we merged 
the two using \texttt{TSEBRA} (\cite{gabriel_tsebra_2021}). As recommended by published 
protocols (\cite{bonizzoni_navigating_2025}), we merged the two annotations by providing 
the hint files (\texttt{--hintfiles}) and final GTF file (\texttt{--gtf}) of each 
individual \texttt{BRAKER} run as inputs to the \texttt{tsebra.py} script. We assessed 
the gene-completeness of the merged annotation using \texttt{compleasm protein}, using 
both the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} reference datasets.
