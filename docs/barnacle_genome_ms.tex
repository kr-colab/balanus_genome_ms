The size of a population is a key factor mediating the mode and tempo of evolution, particularly as it relates to strength of stochastic versus deterministic evolutionary forces. We know from theory that selection is less efficacious in small populations [Kimura1963; Charlesworth2009; Gravel2016], resulting in a reduction in genetic diversity from the stochastic effects of genetic drift and an increase in genetic load due to the accumulations of deleterious mutations [Dussex2023]. These dynamics of evolution in small populations have been extensively supported by empirical observations, e.g., distance to sub-Saharan Africa as a predictor of genetic load in humans [Henn2015], and the increase in genetic load and inbreeding depression in small populations of conservation concern [Tian2022; Mathur2021; Kardos2023]. In contrast, evolution in large populations is thought to be primarily mediated by deterministic processes due to an increase in the efficacy of selection [Charlesworth2009; Gravel2016; Lanfear2014]. Interestingly, this increase in selection can in turn lead to a reduction in genetic diversity due to the hitchhiking of neutral alleles in a stochastic-like fashion [MaynardSmith1974]. In large enough populations, this hitchhiking effect is theorized as the main driver of the stochastic loss of genetic diversity [MaynardSmith1974; Gillespie2000]. Yet, in contrast to small populations, empirical studies of these evolutionary mechanisms has been, with a few key exceptions [Dey2013; Teterina2025], mostly limited to eukaryotic species with small-to-moderate historical population sizes [Cutter2013]. This gap in knowledge highlights the need for empirical studies in systems with historically large population sizes and elevated levels of genetic polymorphism.

The Pacific acorn barnacle (\textit{Balanus glandula}) presents an exceptional model to study the dynamics and mechanism of evolution in large populations. This species is widespread in the intertidal across the Pacific coast of North America, from Baja California to Alaska, where it can reach densities of up to tens of thousands of individuals per square meter [Menge2000]. This species has been the subject of numerous prior studies, with research describing the link between environmental factors and variation in development, recruitment, dispersal, and reproduction [Berger2006; Berger2009; Barshis2011; Nishizaki2014; PfeifferHoyt2005; Schubart1995]. There has also been substantial work regarding the spatial population structure of \textit{B. glandula} along the Pacific coast, particularly with regard to a steep genetic cline in the coast of California that separates populations North and South of this transition point [Sotka2004; Wares2005; Wares2019; Wares2021]. Moreover, \textit{B. glandula} has also been introduced outside its native range, and genetic work has been work to assess invasive populations in Japan [Yorisue2022; Geller2022], Argentina [Geller2022], and South Africa [Simon-Blecher2010].

Previous work on barnacles has shown both substantial levels of genetic diversity [AlmRosenbald2020], as well as complex patterns of selection and local adaptation at small spatial scales [Nunez2020; Nunez2021]. In the northern acorn barnacle (\textit{Semibalanus balanoides}), there is evidence of zonation of adaptive alleles in the mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (\textit{Mpi}) gene between low and high intertidal barnacles less than five meters apart [Nunez2020]. Moreover, while \textit{Mpi} as a whole is under balancing selection across the intertidal, there is evidence for a more complex selection landscape across the gene. Different exons appear to be under varying selection effects across spatial and temporal scales, beyond the low versus high intertidal. While this is a comprehensive look at genetic diversity and selection in a barnacle species, the work primarily focused on a single protein-coding gene. The landscape of genetic diversity and signatures of selection across the \textit{S. balanoides} genome remain unknown.
\vspace{0.2cm}

% AlmRosenbald2020: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10126-021-10033-8

Similarly, while there has been extensive prior genetic work in \textit{B. glandula}, the species also remains genomically understudied. Previous genetic research in the species has been limited to a handful of nuclear and mitochondrial markers, with the most extensive genetic work conducted using reduced representation sequencing in the absence of a reference genome [Wares2021]. Two reference assemblies for \textit{B. glandula} have been deposited to NCBI's GenBank database (NCBI accessions GCA\_030265075.1 and GCA\_049308085.2); however, they both appear to be highly fragmented and/or incomplete. Most publicly available barnacle genomes follow the same fate, being highly-fragmented likely due to the difficulty of assembling highly polymorphic genomes using short reads. While there are a handful of recent high-quality, chromosome-level assemblies for barnacles [Han2024; Bernot2022; Carlson2025; Blaxter2023], they belong to evolutionary distant species, which limits their use for studies in \textit{B. glandula}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

% Han2024: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01733-7
% Bernot2022: https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/giac021
% Carlson2025: https://doi.org/10.1093/jhered/esaf052
% Blaxter2023: https://doi.org/10.12688/wellcomeopenres.18936.1

In this work, we describe a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for the Pacific acorn barnacle. We use this assembly to perform whole-genome comparative analysis, revealing conservation in genome architecture across 165 million years of barnacle evolution. We additionally perform the first assessment of genome-wide genetic variation in this system, revealing extreme levels of nucleotide diversity across the genome, with over 20 million SNPs observed in just six individuals. In accordance to theoretical expectations, we observe a reduction in diversity in protein-coding sequences, reflecting strong background selection and functional constraints in barnacle populations. Overall, our new assembly provides a key resource for future research in this system, setting the stage for \textit{B. glandula} as a model for the study of evolution in large, highly polymorphic populations.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Genome assembly}

% General stats on assembly. Confirming the quality and status of the genome.

We assembled a chromosome-level reference genome for the Pacific acorn barnacle using PacBio Hi-Fi long reads and Hi-C chromosome conformation capture. Preliminary assessment of the PacBio Hi-Fi data using k-mers revealed an expected genome size of around 845 Mbp, with a heterozygosity of over 3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_kmers}). Our initial contig-level assembly, generated using \texttt{hifiasm} [Cheng2021; Cheng2022] with default parameters, generated sequences with a total length of 1.64 Gbp, composed of 4,212 fragments, and a contig N50 of 734 kbp. We also assessed gene-completeness using \texttt{BUSCO}s [Manni2021; Tegenfeldt2024], as implemented in \texttt{compleasm} [Huang\&Li2023], resulting in a completeness of 95.66\% (\texttt{S}:45.31\%; \texttt{D}:50.35\%; \texttt{F}:0.79\%; \texttt{M}:3.55\%; \texttt{N}:1,013) for the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} single-copy ortholog set (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). The statistics for this initial assembly, with twice the expected genome size and over 50\% duplicated \texttt{BUSCO}s, suggested the presence of a large fraction of haplotig sequences, reflecting the high heterozygosity observed for the species.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following optimization, both in the integration of additional data types and in the increased stringency of the purging of haplotig and contaminant sequences (see Methods), we generated a contig-level assembly composed of 1,342 sequences, with a total length of 1.05 Gbp, a contig N50 of 1.35 Mbp, and a \texttt{BUSCO} completeness of 93.88\% ([\texttt{S}:89.04\%; \texttt{D}:4.84\%; \texttt{F}:1.18\%; \texttt{M}:4.94\%; \texttt{N}:1,013], \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10}). The contig-level assembly was then scaffolded with Hi-C data using the \texttt{YAHS} software [Zhou2022] (Figure~\ref{fig:sup_hic}). Our scaffolding process generated an assembly with a total length of 1.05 Gbp, composed of 650 scaffolds, a scaffold N50 of 50.96 Mbp, and a \texttt{BUSCO} completeness of 93.98\% ([\texttt{S}:89.24\%; \texttt{D}:4.74\%; \texttt{F}:0.89\%; \texttt{M}:5.13\%; \texttt{N}:1,013], \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10}) (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}).
\vspace{0.2cm}

This scaffold-level assembly was further processed by performing manual curation of the Hi-C contact map and self-correction using the software \texttt{Inspector} [Chen2021]. This curated assembly was composed of 592 fragments, a total sequence length of 1.04 Gbp, a scaffold N50 of 51.41 Mbp, and a per-base quality value (QV) of 30.49. When assessing completeness, the curated assembly was 94.08\% (\texttt{S}:89.44\%; \texttt{D}:4.64\%; \texttt{F}:0.89\%; \texttt{M}:5.03\%; \texttt{N}:1,013) and 92.77\% (\texttt{S}:87.77\%; \texttt{D}:5.40\%; \texttt{F}:2.93\%; \texttt{M}:4.30\%; \texttt{N}:1,536) gene-complete for the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} \texttt{BUSCO} datasets, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:sup_asm_stats}). Of the 1.04 Gbp of total sequence, 906.6 Mbp (86.74\%) was contained in 58 scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp. Moreover, the 16 largest sequences (larger than 8.5 Mbp) contain 841.2 Mbp (80.61\%) of the total sequence, and likely represent 16 putative chromosomes. This karyotype number, N=16, is in agreement with that observed for the genus \textit{Balanus} [Austin1958] and other acorn barnacle species in the family Balanidae [Han2024].

\subsection*{Genome annotation}

% Stats and QC on the annotation.

Once the assembly and scaffolding was completed, we annotated and masked repeat sequences in the \textit{B. glandula} reference assembly using the \texttt{EarlGrey} software [Baril2024]. This analysis identified 698.6 Mbp (66.94\%) of the assembly as repetitive elements (Table~\ref{tab:sup_earlgrey_summary}); however, the majority of these, 404.5 Mbp (38.76\% of the assembly), remain unclassified among the major repeat element types. This large proportion of unclassified repeat elements is likely the product of the very small number of representative barnacle sequences among curated repeat reference libraries, reflecting barnacle's status as a genomically understudied group. Among the classified repeats, DNA and LINE elements were the two most abundant repeats classes, comprising 8.45\% and 9.16\% of the total assembly, respectively.
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following repeat annotation and masking of the reference genome, we annotated protein-coding genes using short-read RNAseq and long-read PacBio Iso-Seq sequencing, combining and curating annotations generated across the two data types (see Methods). This process resulted in 20,234 protein-coding genes annotated in the genome. The resulting annotation was 93.58\% (\texttt{S}:79.76\%; \texttt{D}:13.82\%; \texttt{F}:1.78\%; \texttt{M}:4.64\%; \texttt{N}:1,013) and 84.77\% (\texttt{S}:72.66\%; \texttt{D}:12.11\%; \texttt{F}:4.10\%; \texttt{M}:11.13\%; \texttt{N}:1,536) gene-complete when compared against the \texttt{arthropoda\_odb10} and \texttt{crustacea\_odb12} \texttt{BUSCO} datasets, respectively. Additionally, we compared our annotation against the proteome of four other barnacle species (see Methods) using \texttt{OrthoFinder} [Emms\&Kelly2015; Emms\&Kelly2019], resulting in the assignment of 16,978 (83.91\%) \textit{B. glandula} genes to orthologous groups across barnacles and the identification of 2,576 single-copy orthologs across all five species.
\vspace{0.2cm}

In addition to protein-coding genes, we also annotated non-coding elements in the \textit{B. glandula} reference assembly. We identified 7,532 long, non-coding RNAs (lncRNA), 1,203 transfer RNAs (tRNA), and 24 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. In total, across protein-coding and non-coding transcripts, we were able to annotate 28,993 genes in the genome.

\subsection*{Conserved architecture across barnacle genomes}

% Just touching on synteny. Things are fairly clean, so not much to say for now.

We performed a conserved synteny analysis using the software \texttt{Genespace} [Lovell2022] to identify patterns of large-scale structure across barnacle genomes. We compared our \textit{B. glandula} genome against the chromosome-level genome assemblies of the striped barnacle \textit{Amphibalanus amphitrite} (Balanidae) [Han2024] and the gooseneck barnacle \textit{Pollicipes pollicipes} (Pollicipedidae) [Bernot2022]. These genomes display conservation in their large-scale organization, including correspondence between 16 one-to-one orthologous chromosomes across the three species (Figure~\ref{fig:synteny}). This finding both shows that our scaffolding of the \textit{B. glandula} assembly likely reflects the biological structure of the genome and is not the product of technical artifacts, but also indicates the conservation in the genome architecture of barnacle genomes across $\sim$165 Mya of evolution [Han 2024; Bernot2022].

\subsection*{Diversity in the barnacle reference assembly}

% Talking about the diversity on just our reference sample. There is tons of variation on just one individual, so I thought it was important just to say that. We delve more on this on the later popgen section, but this individual is not included there.

We calculated estimates of diversity present in the barnacle genome by genotyping our \textit{B. glandula} reference individual. After filtering low-quality genotypes and masking regions with annotated repeats, we obtained genotypes for 314 million sites along the genome, equivalent to 34.71\% of the 904.94 Mbp present in the 58 scaffolds larger than 1 Mbp (Table~\ref{tab:sup_ref_var_stats}). Among these 314 million genotyped sites, 9.08 million (2.89\%) were heterozygous in the reference individual, including 8.73 million SNPs and 356 thousand indels. In total, this variation represent a mean density of 14.66 heterozygous sites per kbp (median: 6; standard deviation: 19.86). Moreover, we observed a total of 399 thousand variant sites within the span of protein-coding sequences, indicating a substantial proportion of coding variants in just a single barnacle individual.

\subsection*{Conservation of functional elements in barnacle genomes}

% For phastCons, mostly saying how we found the hughly conserved stuff and what it is. The enrichment results are generally expected (tRNAs and CDSs are conserved). Not sure what else to say there.

We used the \texttt{phastCons} software [Siepel2005] on the \texttt{cactus} whole-genome alignment [Armstrong2020] between six barnacle species to identify highly conserved sites in the Pacific acorn barnacle genome. Across the 16 putative chromosomes in the \textit{B. glandula} assembly, \texttt{phastCons} identified 196,141 highly-conserved elements (HCEs), spanning 50.85 Mbp of sequence and 4.92\% of the assembled genome. In \textit{B. glandula}, the HCEs had a mean length of 259.25 bp (median: 42 bp, standard deviation: 990.40 bp).
\vspace{0.2cm}

Following the identification of HCEs, we compared their sequence composition against the genome-wide background (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}). For this, we first overlaid the genomic coordinates of the HCEs against the \textit{B. glandula} annotation, obtaining the proportion of annotated features among HCE sites (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}A). Among our target features, including an ``other'' and ``multiple'' class for the unannotated fraction of the genome and annotation overlaps, respectively, all but one is present among HCEs. We do not observe any rRNA sites among our conserved sites; however, this is likely the product of the whole-genome alignment, given rRNA's repetitive nature and alignment difficulty [e.g., Gillispie2004], and not an accurate measure of conservation. Aside from the absence of rRNAs, the sequence composition of HCE largely follows the composition of the whole genome. Most annotated features do not appear substantially enriched among HCE sites (Figure~\ref{fig:phastcons}B, Table~\ref{tab:sup_phastcons_enrichment}). The two clear exceptions are coding sequences (CDSs) and tRNAs. CDSs display an average two-fold enrichment among HCEs (mean: 1.96, median: 1.98, standard deviation: 0.846), reflecting the conservation of protein-coding genes among barnacle genomes. Similarly, tRNAs are five-fold enriched in HCEs (mean: 4.99, median: 4.10, standard deviation:), the likely product of the strong functional constraint expected for these sequences.

\subsection*{Analysis of molecular evolution}

To look at the rate of molecular evolution in protein coding sequences, we calculated at the pairwise rate of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S}) between \textit{B. glandula} and its congener, the wrinkled barnacle \textit{Balanus crenatus}. We identified and aligned 9,082 single-copy orthologs between both species. The mean pairwise d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} was 0.206 (median: 0.158, standard deviation: 0.191). This ratio indicates that, on average, protein coding sequencing in these barnacle genomes are under purifying selection, reflecting the expected evolutionary constraints over sequences of functional importance. Of the 9 thousand genes assessed, 70 (0.77\%) displayed a d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} $>$ 1, and are likely candidates under positive selection.

\subsection*{Molecular evolution stuff}

One important thing to note that that all of our analyses are based on the whole gene. However, in barnacles there is evidence for selection acting at the single-exon level, which can result in different exons of a single gene being under different selection regimes, e.g., \textit{Mpi} in \textit{S. balanoides} [Nunez2020]. It would be good to explore the patterns of molecular evolution (e.g., d\textsubscript{N}/d\textsubscript{S} and McDonald-Kreitman test) at the level of the single exon to better uncover these patterns. Moreover, we expect the population-scaled recombination rate ($\rho = 4N_{e}r$) to be very high, as a function of the large effective population size in the species. This means that selection and diversity would break down at very short scales along the genome.

\subsection*{Diversity and population size}

Based on the observed diversity we have N\textsubscript{e} of 10\textsuperscript{6}. However, we based on the density of 10\textsuperscript{4} per m\textsuperscript{2}, the census size of the population in OR should be in the billions (if not more) barnacles. Why this disparity? This is a key example of Lewontin's paradox. This species might be a great system to study the mechanisms underlying this disparity between diversity and census size.
